## 1. Purpose, scope and who may do what

This procedure covers what happens from the moment a customer tells us something is wrong with a garment to the moment the matter is closed and filed. It applies at both sites and to every complaint about a garment, whether or not the customer uses the word "claim", and whether the garment is worth $40 or $3,200.

It does not cover complaints about price, turnaround or service, which Sharon handles at her discretion and does not need this procedure for. It starts the moment a garment is put on the counter and something is said to be wrong with it.

### 1.1 Who does what

| Role | Authority |
|---|---|
| **Counter staff** — Emma, Chloe, Rebecca, and Sharon when she is on the front | Take the report. Calm the customer. Record the facts. Take the garment in and hold it. **No admission of liability, no denial of liability, no offer of money, no estimate of blame.** |
| **Sharon Delaney, Shop Manager** | Assesses. Pulls the docket, the intake record, the photographs, the machine log and the route sheet. Examines the garment. Forms a recommendation under clause 6. May settle up to **$250** and may authorise a re-clean, a re-press or an alteration at our cost without reference to Karen. |
| **Peter Nguyen, plant operator** | Provides the plant facts — what machine, what programme, what load, what the log says. Does not decide fault and does not speak to the customer about it. |
| **Anh Tran, alterations** | Advises whether a repair, re-hem, re-line or re-bead is possible and what it would cost us. |
| **Karen Whitfield, Owner** | Decides **anything over $250**, **anything that goes out in writing**, anything involving the insurer, anything involving Consumer Affairs Victoria, and anything where Sharon and the customer are stuck. |

**Anything in writing is Karen's.** A letter, an email that sets out a position, a text message that concedes or refuses anything — all of it goes out over Karen's name, in the house voice, and none of it is sent by anyone else. A friendly email saying "we'll have a look and get back to you" is fine and is not a position.

### 1.2 The two standing principles

1. **Nobody admits or denies liability at the counter.** Not the counter staff, not Sharon, not Peter if he happens to walk through. The counter takes the report. The assessment decides the position. Those are two different jobs done by two different people at two different times, and collapsing them is how a $120 problem becomes a $900 one.
2. **The customer is told the truth, once we know it.** When we are at fault we say so plainly and in the first paragraph. We do not hedge, we do not write "we regret that you feel", and we do not make people fight for what they are owed. That is a commercial decision as much as a moral one: the customers we settle honestly come back, and the ones we grind down go to Consumer Affairs Victoria.

## 2. The first ninety seconds

Somebody puts a garment on the counter and they are unhappy. Perhaps very unhappy. What happens in the next minute and a half decides whether this is a twenty-minute matter or a six-week one.

### 2.1 What to do

**Stop what you are doing.** Put down the hangers. If there is a queue, acknowledge it — *"I'll be with you shortly"* to the queue — and then give this customer your attention.

**Take the garment.** Physically take it, lay it on the bench and look at it while they talk. Looking at the garment while they speak signals that you are taking it seriously, and it starts the assessment.

**Let them finish.** Do not interrupt, do not correct a fact, and do not start explaining. People who are angry about a garment are usually angry about something else as well, and they need to get to the end of it. It takes less time than you think.

**Say the three things.** In this order:

> *"I'm sorry — that's not what you should have got back from us."*
>
> *"Let me write down exactly what's happened, and then I'll have Sharon look at it properly."*
>
> *"Can I keep the garment here so she can see it? I'll give you a receipt for it."*

The first sentence is an apology for the *situation*, not an admission of fault, and it is the single most useful thing anybody says in this procedure. "That's not what you should have got back from us" is true whether the fault is ours, the manufacturer's or nobody's.

**Move them off the front counter if you can.** At Bendigo, the end of the counter by the alterations window. It takes the audience away and drops the temperature immediately.

**Get the garment onto the hold rail.** Bagged, tagged with the docket number and the date, not touched again until clause 4 and 5 have happened.

### 2.2 What must never be said

These six phrases are prohibited. Not discouraged — prohibited. Each of them has cost this business money.

1. **"You signed for it."** The at-risk authorisation is not a waiver, it does not exclude the consumer guarantees, and saying this makes us look as though we think it does. It also tells the customer that we knew there was a risk and are now hiding behind a signature, which is the opposite of why the form exists.
2. **"That's not our fault."** Said before anyone has assessed anything, it is a guess, and if the assessment goes the other way we have to climb back down from it. Even when it turns out to be true, it is the wrong sentence — the right one is a technical explanation, later, in writing.
3. **"That's just wear and tear."** It might be. But it is a finding, not a greeting, and it sounds like a brush-off delivered across a counter.
4. **"The label said dry clean, so there's nothing we could have done."** Following a care label is not automatically a defence, and this sentence gives away the whole assessment before it has been made.
5. **"You'll have to take that up with the manufacturer."** Even where the fault is a manufacturing one, this is our customer standing in front of us. We are the ones who write the letter they take back to the retailer. We do not send people away.
6. **"Everyone gets that with this fabric."** If everybody gets it, we should have said so at intake, and we did not.

Two more that are not prohibited but should be avoided: *"I'll have to ask my manager"* (say *"Sharon will look at it herself"* instead, which sounds like an assessment rather than a delay), and *"we've never had this happen before"* (unverifiable, and irrelevant to them).

### 2.3 Taking the heat out

- **Lower your voice.** People match volume. It works every time and it costs nothing.
- **Use their name** once you have it from the docket.
- **Agree with what is true.** *"You're right, that mark wasn't there on the docket."* Agreeing with a true thing early buys enormous credit for disagreeing with something later.
- **Give a next step with a time on it.** *"Sharon's in tomorrow from seven. She'll look at it in the morning and I'll have her ring you before midday."* Vagueness is what makes people escalate.
- **Do not defend the plant.** You do not know yet, and it is not your job.
- **If they will not calm down**, do not argue and do not match them. *"I can see how frustrating this is. Let me get the details down and get it to Sharon today."* If they are abusive, Sharon or Karen takes over; nobody on this counter has to absorb that.

### 2.4 The rule

**Nobody admits or denies liability at the counter.** Say what we will do next and when. Do not say who is at fault, because you do not know, and the person who has to unsay it is Karen.

## 3. Taking the report

Form MSDC-F10, the problem report, is raised in the app while the customer is still there. Not afterwards.

### 3.1 The fields, and why each one matters

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Docket number | Everything else hangs off it — the intake record, the route sheet, the machine log, the photographs. Without it there is no assessment. |
| Customer name and contact | We will need to ring them, probably more than once. |
| Site | Which counter took the garment in, and which counter is taking the complaint. They are often different. |
| Date reported, and by whom | Fixes the timeline and starts the clock on our own response times. |
| Date cleaned | The gap between cleaning and complaint is itself evidence. A complaint on collection day and a complaint six weeks later are different animals. |
| Garment description | Type, colour, brand, style, and anything that identifies this garment from any other. |
| Care label as it reads now | Read it again yourself. Do not copy the intake record — check whether it agrees. |
| What happened, in the customer's words | Clause 3.2. |
| What you can see | Your own observation, kept separate from theirs. |
| Where the damage is, and how big | Location, size in millimetres, and whether it is on one part or all over. |
| Prior cleaning history | By us, and elsewhere. Ask. Damage from a previous clean by somebody else surfaces on the second clean surprisingly often. |
| Value, and where that figure comes from | "It cost $1,250 at David Jones" is a fact. "It's worth about a thousand" is an opinion. Record which one you were given. |
| Purchase date and place | Needed for Table I and Table II under clause 8. Ask for the receipt if they have it; most people do not, and that is fine. |
| What the customer wants | Clause 3.3. |
| Whether an authorisation exists | Look it up while they are there. |
| Photographs taken today | Clause 4. |
| Where the garment is now | Hold rail, bagged, tagged. |

### 3.2 Writing "what happened" so it is useful

Two accounts go into the report and they are kept apart.

**The customer's account, in their words.** As close to verbatim as you can manage. Not tidied up, not summarised into "customer alleges damage".

> *"She says the jacket went in with the trousers on 22 May, she collected on 27 May, and she noticed on the Saturday when she went to wear it that the collar and both shoulders are lighter than the rest. She says it's obvious in daylight and she can't wear it to work."*

Their words matter because their story rarely changes and any change in it later is significant, and because a customer who reads back their own words feels heard.

**Your account, in yours.** What you can see on the bench, in the daylight lamp, described the way clause 5 of SOP 01 describes anything:

> *"Collar and the tops of both shoulders are visibly lighter than the body of the jacket and than the trousers. There is a defined line across the shoulder seam. Colour change is even on both sides. No fibre damage visible, no thinning, no marking."*

Keep the two apart in the record. When Sharon assesses, she needs to know which parts she is being told and which parts have been observed.

### 3.3 What the customer actually wants

Ask, and write down the answer in their words:

> *"What would you like us to do about it?"*

It is a genuine question and the answer is frequently much smaller than anybody assumed. Two out of three people want the garment put right, or a clean redone, or an apology and an explanation they can understand. The minority who want money often want less than the full replacement price. You cannot settle a complaint you have not asked the shape of.

Record it exactly. *"Wants it replaced — says it cost $890 at Myer in October 2023."* *"Wants the mark out, doesn't want money."* *"Wants to know how it happened."* *"Wants a refund of the cleaning charge and nothing else."* Do not translate a request for an explanation into a claim for compensation. That is how small things become big ones.

Do not promise the outcome. What you say is:

> *"I've written that down and Sharon will have it in the morning. I can't tell you today what we'll be able to do, but somebody will ring you by tomorrow afternoon either way."*

## 4. Do not touch it yet

**The garment is not re-cleaned, spotted, pressed, brushed, steamed or worked on in any way until it has been assessed under clause 5 and photographed.** Not by the counter, not by the plant, not by anybody being helpful.

This is the rule most often broken with the best intentions, and it is the most expensive one to break.

**Why:**

- **Re-cleaning destroys the evidence.** Once a garment has been through a second cycle nobody can say what the first one did. Every subsequent conversation — with the customer, with Karen, with the insurer, with a manufacturer, with Consumer Affairs Victoria — becomes an argument about a garment that no longer exists in the condition complained of.
- **It usually makes it worse.** A delaminated fusible does not re-bond. A cloudy bead does not re-clarify. Colour that has migrated migrates further. A shrunken wool jumper shrinks again. Of the failure modes we see, the number that improve with a second clean is close to zero.
- **It removes the comparison.** Clause 5.4 depends on comparing an affected area with an unaffected area of the same garment. A second full clean often changes both.
- **It looks like concealment.** A garment that has been quietly re-processed before anybody photographed it is a bad fact, and it is a bad fact even when nothing was being concealed.
- **It pre-empts the decision.** Re-cleaning is a remedy. Offering a remedy before assessing is an admission, and it is Sharon's decision or Karen's, not the counter's.

**What to do instead.** Bag it, tag it with the docket number and today's date, and put it on the hold rail. Photograph it today, under clause 4.1. Then leave it alone.

### 4.1 Photographs at the point of complaint

Taken the same day, on the site tablet, into the app against the docket, alongside the intake photographs if there are any.

1. The whole garment, front and back, flat, in daylight.
2. The damage, close, with the yellow 150 mm scale card in frame.
3. The same detail on an unaffected part of the same garment, for comparison — the underside of a collar, an inside seam, a facing, the trousers of the same suit.
4. The care label, again, as it reads today.
5. Any maker's or retailer's label, and any size or fibre-content label.

The comparison shot at point 3 is the one people forget and the one that decides the most claims. A photograph of a lightened collar means little on its own; the same photograph beside the unchanged trousers of the same suit means a great deal.

### 4.2 The one permitted exception

If the garment is wet, contaminated or actively deteriorating — a dye that is still bleeding, mould that is growing — Sharon may direct a stabilising step. She rings Karen first, the reason is written on the assessment record, and photographs are taken before and after. That is the only exception, and it is a direction from Sharon, never an initiative from the counter or the plant.

## 5. The assessment

Sharon assesses. The assessment is a paper exercise and a physical one, and it is recorded on Form MSDC-F11.

### 5.1 What to pull, and what each thing tells you

| Record | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **The docket** | Who took it, at which site, on what date, what service was asked for, what was promised, what was charged. It also tells you who to ask, because the person who inspected it is named on it. |
| **The intake record** (SOP 01 clause 5) | The condition of the garment before we touched it. Pre-existing damage, worn areas, previous repairs, existing stains and their class, whether the pockets were emptied, whether the belt came off. This is the most valuable single record we hold, and its absence is itself a finding. |
| **The intake photographs** | The same thing, but unarguable. Compare like with like — the same panel, the same seam, the same trim. |
| **The care label as recorded at intake** | What we were permitted to do. Circle, letter, bars, crosses. Then check it against the label on the garment now, because a mis-read label at intake is a real cause of real damage. |
| **The route sheet** | What the counter told the plant to do, and any special instruction — belt removed, run alone, do not press, collar pre-spot. If the instruction is not on the sheet, it did not reach the plant. |
| **The machine log** | What the plant actually did. Machine, programme number, load composition, cycle length, bath time, extract speed, drying temperature, cool-down, operator, and the time and date. The Union XL-800 and the Realstar HM-450 both log by cycle; the Speed Queens log programme and temperature. This is where a garment run at 40 °C on a programme that should have been 30 °C shows up, in black and white, with nobody's memory involved. |
| **Any MSDC-F02 authorisation** | Whether the risk that eventuated is the risk that was named, and — critically — whether it was signed before the garment was accepted. |
| **The customer's file** | Prior work, prior complaints, prior settlements. Relevant to context, never to the merits. |

Read the machine log before you form a view. It is the only record in the building that nobody had an opinion while making.

### 5.2 Examining the garment

In daylight — the north window at the Bendigo counter in the morning, or the 6,500 K daylight lamp. Never under the plant fluoros, which flatten colour and hide exactly the differences you are looking for.

Flat on the bench, right way out first, then inside out. Handle it as little as possible and do not brush, pick or rub at anything.

Work the same order as SOP 01 clause 4.2 — collar, shoulders, cuffs, front, back, hem, lining, pockets, closures, trims — because you are looking for what *else* is going on as much as for the thing complained of.

### 5.3 What you are looking at

Distinguish between these, because they have different causes and different outcomes:

- **Colour change** — lighter, darker, duller, yellowed, or shifted in hue. Even or uneven. Bounded by a line, or graduated.
- **Dimensional change** — shrinkage, stretching, twisting, distortion of a panel or a hem.
- **Surface change** — pilling, matting, loss of nap, shine, glazing, loss of a finish or coating.
- **Structural failure** — a split seam, a hole, a tear, a broken thread, delamination.
- **Deposit or transfer** — colour that has arrived from somewhere else, a redeposited soil, a lint or fibre transfer.
- **Component failure** — a bead, a sequin, a button, a zip, a buckle, an interfacing. The garment is fine and the part is not.

Name it in those terms on the assessment record. "Ruined" is not a finding; *"uneven colour loss across the collar and both shoulder tops, bounded by a defined line at the shoulder seam, no fibre damage"* is.

### 5.4 Comparing against an unaffected part

This is the technique that decides more assessments than any other, and it takes two minutes.

Find a part of the *same garment* that has not been affected and compare it, side by side, in the same light. The underside of a collar against its top. An inside facing against an outer panel. A pocket flap against the body. The trousers of the same suit against the jacket. A seam allowance against the face fabric.

What the comparison tells you:

- **Uniform change across the whole garment** points to the process — solvent, temperature, mechanical action.
- **Change only where the garment is exposed to wear, light or contact** points to wear, sun or use, not to us. Collar, cuffs, shoulders, elbows and seat are the wear points.
- **Change bounded by a hard line** points to something that was applied or deposited on the garment — a spill, a residue, a previous treatment — and not to the bath.
- **Change on one component only** points to that component.

Photograph the comparison. It is the shot that persuades a customer, and it is the shot that persuades an insurer.

## 6. Where does fault sit

Six questions, in this order. Answer them on the assessment record before you go anywhere near a number.

**Question 1 — Was the care label followed?**
Read the circle, the letter and the bars as recorded at intake, and check what the machine log says we did. If the label permitted F and we ran P, stop: that is our failure and the answer is settle. If the label permitted P and we ran P at the standard process where a bar required a reduced one, stop: same answer.

**Question 2 — Was the method actually gentler than the label allowed?**
This is the strongest defensive fact we have and it is worth being precise about. A garment labelled **P** that we ran in hydrocarbon on the beaded programme — four minute bath, extract capped at 200 rpm, dried at 38 °C with an extended cool-down, run alone rather than in a load — was cleaned materially more gently than its own label permitted. If the garment failed anyway, it did not fail because of what we chose.

**Question 3 — Was there an at-risk authorisation, and does it cover this?**
Three separate sub-questions. Does an MSDC-F02 exist? Was it signed *before* the garment was accepted? And does it name *this* failure mode — not damage in general, but this? An authorisation that names clouding of the beading and a garment whose beading has clouded is a strong position. An authorisation that says "damage may occur" is not. An authorisation signed at collection is worth nothing at all and is not to be relied on in any letter (SOP 01 clause 9.3).

**Question 4 — Is the failure mode a known fabric or manufacturing fault?**
Some things fail in cleaning because of how they were made, and the industry literature says so. Plastic bead coatings, fusible interfacings, applied prints, PU coatings, dye that was never fixed properly, a trim the care label never accounted for. Where the DLI TABS bulletins cover the mechanism, name the relevant bulletin by title on the assessment record and quote what it attributes the failure to. Where the care label did not account for a trim or a component, that is non-compliance with AS/NZS 1957 and it is the manufacturer's problem, not ours.

**Question 5 — Is it wear?**
Age, use, abrasion, sun, perspiration and previous cleaning all show up in the first clean after they have accumulated. Wear presents at the wear points, gradually, and shows on both sides symmetrically where both sides get the same use. Cleaning reveals wear; it does not cause it. But be careful with this answer, because it is the easiest one to reach for and the easiest to be wrong about. Do not give it unless the pattern of the damage supports it and the comparison at clause 5.4 backs it up.

**Question 6 — Is it something we did?**
The honest question, asked last so that it is asked properly. Was the collar built up and sent to perc untreated? Was the belt left on? Did the programme run at the wrong temperature? Was it pressed when the route sheet said not to? Was it packed in ordinary tissue instead of acid-free? The machine log and the route sheet answer most of these and they answer them without regard to anybody's feelings.

If the answer is yes, the answer is yes. We say so, we say so first, and we do not make the customer prove it.

### 6.1 The four outcomes

The six questions lead to one of four outcomes.

| Outcome | When it applies |
|---|---|
| **Defend** | Not our fault, and the fault sits with the garment, its manufacture, its trim or its labelling. We say so technically and we help the customer take it back to whoever sold it. |
| **Deny** | Not our fault, and there is nobody else to send them to — the damage is wear, age or an inherent characteristic of the cloth. We decline liability, explain why, and where we can, offer something practical that costs us little and helps them. |
| **Settle** | Our fault. We accept it, we calculate the adjusted value under clause 8, and we pay. |
| **Goodwill** | Nothing is damaged and nobody is at fault, but the customer has not had what they expected. We put it right at our cost and we do not treat it as a claim. |

### 6.2 The worked flow

1. Is the garment actually damaged, as opposed to not-as-expected? If not damaged, go to **goodwill**.
2. Did we depart from the care label, the route sheet or our own procedure? If yes, go to **settle**.
3. Does the machine log show a process error? If yes, go to **settle**.
4. Is the failure mode a known manufacturing, trim or labelling fault? If yes, and there is a retailer or maker to go back to, go to **defend**.
5. Is it wear, age or an inherent characteristic? If yes, go to **deny**, and look for something practical to offer.
6. Is there a signed authorisation, given before acceptance, naming this failure mode, and was the method at least as gentle as the label allowed? If yes, go to **defend**.
7. If you are genuinely between two outcomes, take it to Karen. The tie does not go to the business.

## 7. The Australian Consumer Law position

Everybody at this counter should be able to say this much accurately, because customers frequently say a version of it that is not quite right, and because it is the framework the whole procedure sits inside.

### 7.1 Section 60 — due care and skill

Under section 60 of the Australian Consumer Law, a supplier of services to a consumer guarantees that the services will be rendered **with due care and skill**.

What that means for us is that we promise a **competent job**, not a **perfect outcome**. It is a guarantee about the standard of our work — the method chosen, the process run, the attention given — and not a warranty that every garment will emerge exactly as it went in. If we identify the fibre correctly, read the label correctly, choose an appropriate method, run it correctly, and the garment fails anyway because of how it was made, we have met the guarantee.

Conversely, if we run a garment labelled F in perchloroethylene, we have not exercised due care and skill, and no amount of anything else will fix that.

### 7.2 A sign or a docket cannot exclude it

The consumer guarantees cannot be excluded, restricted or modified by contract. A sign on the wall, a line of small print on the docket, or a signature on Form MSDC-F02 does not remove the guarantee of due care and skill, and any attempt to say it does is itself a breach.

This is why nobody says *"you signed for it"* (clause 2.2). The authorisation is evidence that a risk was identified and explained and that the customer chose to proceed. It is good evidence and it decides real cases. What it is not, and never claims to be on its face, is a waiver.

### 7.3 Section 61 — fitness for a stated purpose

Section 61 adds a guarantee that where a consumer makes known the particular purpose for which the services are being acquired, or the result they want to achieve, the services and any product resulting from them will be reasonably fit for that purpose or of such a nature that they can reasonably be expected to achieve that result.

At our counter this bites whenever a customer tells us *why* they are having something cleaned and we take the work on that footing. *"I need this for a wedding on Saturday."* *"I want the mark on the back out — that's the whole reason I'm here."* *"This is going into a box for my daughter."*

Two consequences follow, both practical. First, **do not accept a stated purpose you cannot meet**: if the mark will not come out, say so at intake and write on the docket that you said so (SOP 01 clause 5.5). Second, **record the stated purpose** when there is one, because it changes what we have promised.

### 7.4 What a "major failure" means here

A failure is major, broadly, where a reasonable consumer would not have acquired the service had they known the extent of the problem, where the service is substantially unfit for its normal purpose or for the purpose made known and cannot easily be made fit within a reasonable time, or where the service creates an unsafe situation.

Where a failure is major, the consumer chooses the remedy — cancelling the service and getting a refund, or compensation for the drop in value. Where the failure is **not** major, the supplier chooses, and we may remedy it in a reasonable time by putting it right.

In our trade the practical line usually falls like this. A garment we have damaged so that it cannot reasonably be worn as it was — colour lost, shrunk out of size, delaminated — is a major failure, the customer chooses, and clause 8 tells us what the compensation figure is. A garment where a mark did not come out, or which needs re-cleaning or re-pressing, is normally not a major failure, and our remedy is to do it again properly, promptly and at our cost.

Never argue about whether something is major at the counter. Assess it, decide, and put the position in writing over Karen's name.

## 8. The International Fair Claims Guide

### 8.1 What it is and why we use it

The International Fair Claims Guide for Consumer Textile Products is the schedule the dry cleaning industry uses worldwide to put a value on a damaged garment. It is not law and it does not bind a customer. We use it for three reasons: it is consistent, so the same garment gets the same answer whoever assesses it; it is external, so the number is not something we invented to suit ourselves; and it is explainable, so a customer can see the working.

It has two parts. **Table I** gives an expected **life expectancy** for each class of article. **Table II** gives a **percentage of replacement cost** to be paid, according to how much of that life has been used and what condition the garment was in.

The principle is depreciated value, not replacement cost. A customer is entitled to be put back where they were — which is the value of a part-worn garment, not the price of a brand new one. Nobody enjoys hearing this, so the arithmetic must be shown, plainly, every time.

### 8.2 Table I — life expectancy

Life expectancy is the period over which an article of that class is expected to remain serviceable with normal use and care. Some representative entries:

| Article | Life expectancy |
|---|---|
| Suit — wool or wool blend | 3 years |
| Dress — fancy | 2 years |
| Dress — plain, everyday | 3 years |
| Jumper or cardigan — wool | 4 years |
| Overcoat — wool | 5 years |
| Wedding gown — after the wedding | 3 years |
| Leather or suede jacket | 4 years |
| Curtains and drapes, lined | 5 years |

Life expectancy is not a use-by date. A garment older than its life expectancy has not become worthless; it has become fully depreciated for the purposes of the Guide, and the settlement reflects that.

### 8.3 Table II — depreciation

Table II reads across two axes: how far into the life expectancy the garment is, expressed as an age band, and the condition it was in before we touched it — excellent, average or poor. The intersection gives the percentage of replacement cost payable.

The condition column is where the intake record earns its keep. "Excellent" and "average" are not opinions offered at the point of complaint; they are findings that ought to be supported by what was written on the docket at intake under SOP 01 clause 5.

### 8.4 A worked example

A wool jumper. Replacement cost $640, established from the customer's receipt.

1. **Table I.** Jumper or cardigan, wool — life expectancy **4 years**.
2. **Age.** Purchased 14 months before the incident. The applicable age band is **13 to 25 months**.
3. **Condition at intake.** The intake record shows no pilling, no thinning at the elbows, no previous repairs. Condition **excellent**.
4. **Table II.** The intersection of that band and that condition gives **70%**.
5. **Adjustment.** 70% × $640 = **$448.00**.
6. **The cleaning charge.** The $24.00 charged for the clean is **waived**, and the waiver is stated in the letter. We do not charge for work that produced this outcome, and the waiver is not part of the settlement figure — it is in addition to it.
7. **The offer.** $448.00, plus the waived charge.

The customer will observe that $448 does not buy the jumper again. That is correct and it is what depreciated value means, and the answer is not to apologise for the Guide but to show the working:

> *"The jumper was fourteen months into an expected four-year life, and it was in excellent condition, which is why the adjustment is seventy per cent rather than something lower. Settling at replacement cost would put you in a better position than you were in the day before you brought it to us, and that isn't how textile claims are settled anywhere in the trade."*

### 8.5 The 50% cap on a post-wedding gown

A wedding gown cleaned **after** the wedding is capped at **50% of its replacement cost**, whatever Table II otherwise produces. The Guide takes the position that the gown has served the purpose for which it was bought.

Worked through: a gown with a replacement cost of $3,200, ten months old, in excellent condition, would ordinarily attract 70% — $2,240. The cap applies, and 50% of $3,200 is **$1,600.00**. The settlement is $1,600.00.

Explain the cap explicitly and never let a customer discover it in the arithmetic. It is the single most upsetting number in this procedure and it needs its own sentence:

> *"There is one more thing I need to explain, because it will look like a deduction and it isn't. The Guide caps a gown cleaned after the wedding at half its replacement cost, on the basis that it has already done what it was bought for. So the seventy per cent becomes the cap, and the figure is $1,600."*

A gown cleaned **before** the wedding is not capped.

### 8.6 The cleaning charge

**The cleaning charge is always waived when we settle.** Every time, without being asked, and it is stated in the letter in a sentence of its own. It is a small amount of money and it does an enormous amount of work: it says we are not charging for a job that went wrong.

The waived charge is not deducted from the adjusted value and is not counted as part of the settlement. If the customer has already paid, it is refunded.

### 8.7 What happens to the garment

On payment of the full adjusted value, **the garment becomes our property**. That is the standard position under the Guide: we have paid its value, so we own it.

In practice we always offer the alternative, and we offer it in the letter rather than waiting to be asked:

> *"On payment the gown becomes ours, which is the usual position — but if you would rather keep it, say so and it's yours. Plenty of people would rather have the garment."*

Where a customer keeps the garment, the settlement is adjusted by agreement to reflect its residual value, and the agreed figure is recorded. Where we take the garment, it is retained for the life of the claim file and not disposed of, worn or sold.

## 9. The four outcomes in practice

### 9.1 Defend

**When.** Not our fault, and there is a manufacturer, retailer or supplier whose problem it properly is. Typically a component or trim failure, a fusible that has delaminated, a coating that has crazed, a dye that was never properly fixed, or a care label that does not account for the garment as made.

**Who signs it off.** Karen. Always, because it goes out in writing.

**What goes out.** A "to whom it may concern" letter addressed to nobody in particular and handed to the customer to take back to the retailer. It confirms the garment was professionally cleaned at our premises, quotes the care label exactly, states the solvent and programme actually used and the precautions applied, explains why the method was gentler than the label permitted where it was, describes the damage in textile language, names the failure mode, cites the relevant DLI TABS bulletin by title, and makes the AS/NZS 1957 point about the label where it applies.

**Tone.** Technical, not apologetic. No admission of liability, no offer of payment. It is not a letter to the customer; it is a letter *for* the customer.

**What is recorded.** The assessment, the letter, and the outcome, against the docket and in the claims register.

### 9.2 Deny

**When.** Not our fault, and there is nobody to send them to. Relaxation shrinkage in curtains. Wear at the collar and cuffs. Sun damage. Age. An inherent characteristic of the cloth.

**Who signs it off.** Karen.

**What goes out.** A letter to the customer. Firm but not cold. It confirms the work and the dates, sets out the measurements or observations that support the finding — including the intake figures — explains the mechanism, cites the relevant DLI TABS bulletin by title, refers to any advice signed at intake, and declines liability plainly.

**And then it offers something.** Wherever we reasonably can, a denial carries a practical offer that costs us little and helps them: Anh letting a hem down at our cost, a re-press, a repair, a free clean next time. A denial with an offer keeps a customer. A denial without one usually does not.

**What is recorded.** As above, plus whatever we offered and whether it was taken up.

### 9.3 Settle

**When.** Our fault, on the six questions at clause 6.

**Who signs it off.** Sharon up to $250. Karen above $250, and Karen for anything going out in writing, which in practice means every settlement.

**What goes out.** A settlement letter that owns it in the first paragraph, plainly, with no hedging and no corporate apology language. Then what happened and why, in plain terms. Then the basis of the offer — the Guide, the life expectancy, the age, the condition, the percentage and the arithmetic, in one short paragraph so the number does not look plucked from the air. Then the offer itself, with the cleaning charge waived. Then the ownership position and the offer to return the garment if they would rather keep it. Then how to accept and how they will be paid.

**How payment is made.** Electronic transfer to the customer's account, or a credit to their account with us if they would rather, within five business days of acceptance. Nadia processes it and it is coded to claims, not to discounts.

**What is recorded.** The assessment, the calculation, the letter, the acceptance, the payment, and the disposition of the garment.

### 9.4 Goodwill

**When.** Nothing is damaged and nobody is at fault, but the customer has not had what they expected. Down that has clumped after cleaning and needs more re-lofting. A crease that has not held. A collar that could be finished better. A promise about turnaround we did not keep.

**Who signs it off.** Sharon, within her $250 authority, without reference to Karen.

**What goes out.** A short, warm letter or a phone call. **No admission of fault and no settlement figure**, because it is not a claim. An explanation of the mechanism, an offer to put it right at our cost, and usually a modest credit on the account for the inconvenience.

**What is recorded.** The problem report and the outcome. It is not entered in the claims register as a claim, because it is not one, and counting goodwill gestures as claims distorts the review at clause 13.

### 10.2 Why most garment claims never reach the insurer

The excess is **$2,500** and almost every garment claim we settle is a long way under it.

A $448 jumper, a $476 dress, a $250 suit and even a $1,600 wedding gown are all paid out of the business, because claiming would cost us the excess and produce nothing. The insurance is not there for a jumper. It is there for the day something happens to a whole load, or to a rack, or to the plant — a machine failure that ruins forty garments at once, a fire, a flood, a theft. That is what the $50,000 any-one-event limit is shaped for.

The practical rule: **anything we might settle at or above $2,500, and any single event that has affected more than a handful of garments, goes to Karen before anything is said to anybody.** Karen calls Hargreaves & Locke.

### 10.3 The notification obligation

The policy requires notification of any **circumstance likely to give rise to a claim**, as soon as reasonably practicable — not just of an actual claim. A written demand, a solicitor's letter, a Consumer Affairs Victoria referral or an incident affecting multiple garments are all notifiable circumstances even before any figure is discussed.

The policy also has the usual condition about **admissions**. Making an admission of liability without the insurer's consent may prejudice cover on a claim that would otherwise be covered.

That sits in tension with clause 1.2, which says we tell the truth and own our mistakes, and the resolution is straightforward and is not a fudge. **Below the excess, the insurer has no interest and we settle honestly and quickly on our own money.** **At or above the excess, nothing is admitted, offered or written until Karen has spoken to the broker.** That is exactly why the authority limit at clause 1.1 exists, and why nobody admits liability at the counter under clause 2.4.

Karen keeps a note in the claims register of every notification, its date, and the broker's reference.

### 11.2 Consumer Affairs Victoria

Consumer Affairs Victoria is the Victorian regulator, and among other things it conciliates disputes between consumers and traders. A customer who is unhappy with our answer is entitled to go to them, and some will. It is not a disaster and it is not an accusation.

If a customer says they are going to Consumer Affairs, the correct response is neither defensive nor dismissive:

> *"That's absolutely your right, and we'll cooperate with them. In the meantime, if it would help, I'll set out our position in writing so you've got it in front of you."*

Never suggest that going to CAV will make things harder for them, and never suggest that it will not help. Both are wrong and one of them is close to intimidation.

### 11.3 What happens when they contact us

CAV will usually write, setting out the consumer's complaint and inviting a response within a stated period. Karen handles it, personally, every time.

**What we provide:**

- a chronology of the job, with dates;
- the docket and the intake record;
- the intake photographs and the complaint photographs;
- the care label as recorded and as it reads;
- the route sheet and the relevant machine log entries;
- any signed MSDC-F02 authorisation, with its date;
- our assessment record and the reasoning at clause 6;
- the settlement calculation where one was made, showing the Table I and Table II working; and
- copies of our correspondence with the customer.

**What we do not provide:** other customers' records, staff personal details, unrelated commercial information, or the contents of internal discussions that are not part of the assessment. If in doubt, Karen asks the broker.

**Tone.** The same as everything else: factual, unemotional, specific, and willing. A file that shows a business which inspected the garment, recorded what it found, explained the risk, chose a method it can justify and offered a remedy on a published industry basis is a file that ends conciliations quickly. That file is built at intake, months earlier, by somebody standing at a counter.

### 11.4 We never ignore a letter

Every letter, email, CAV referral, solicitor's letter or written complaint is acknowledged within **five business days**, and substantively answered within **ten**, even if the answer is that we need longer and here is when we will have it.

Ignoring correspondence turns a garment dispute into a conduct problem. It converts a customer who was cross into a customer who is entitled to be cross, and it looks exactly like what it is. There is no situation in which not replying improves our position.

## 13. Learning from it

### 13.1 The monthly claim review

On the first Tuesday of each month, after close, Karen and Sharon go through every problem report raised in the previous month — settled, defended, denied, goodwill and still open. Peter attends whenever a plant process is involved. It takes about forty minutes.

Four questions per matter:

1. **What actually caused it?** Not who — what.
2. **Where in the chain could it have been caught?** Intake, route sheet, plant, finishing, counter.
3. **Was the record good enough to answer it?** If the assessment was hard, why was it hard?
4. **Does anything need to change?** A procedure, a programme setting, a form, or some training.

The answers go in the claims register against the matter, so that the review is a record and not a conversation.

### 13.2 Three changes we have made as a result

**One — the belt line on the docket.** A dyed self-belt left attached to a dress crocked onto the skirt in the drum and cost us $310. The review found that belts were being counted as part of the garment, so they were never separated at intake. SOP 01 clause 7 now requires every belt, sash and detachable trim to come off at the counter and be docketed as its own piece, and the docket will not close without a line for it. We have not had a belt transfer since.

**Two — built-up collars off the perc route.** A jacket with product build-up on the collar went to perchloroethylene untreated, the residue oxidised, and the collar and shoulders came back lighter than the body. $420. The review established that the build-up had been visible at intake and simply had not been looked for. Collar build-up is now an at-risk feature in its own right under SOP 01 clause 7, the inspection order starts at the collar, and a built-up collar goes to hydrocarbon or is pre-spotted on the board before it goes anywhere near the Union.

**Three — photographs made mandatory at intake.** Two claims in the same quarter turned on the condition of a garment before we cleaned it, and in both we had a written note and no photograph. Both were defensible and neither was easy. From version 4 of SOP 01, effective 1 March 2026, photographs are mandatory for every at-risk garment, every garment on an authorisation, every household textile job and every send-out, with a minimum of six shots and a scale card in frame. The assessment at clause 5 now begins with the intake photographs, and the difference in how quickly a matter can be answered is obvious.

## Appendix A — Assessment checklist (Form MSDC-F11)

Worked in order. Do not skip to the outcome.

1. Problem report complete, including the customer's own words and what they want.
2. Garment on the hold rail, bagged, tagged, **not re-processed**.
3. Complaint photographs taken, including a comparison shot of an unaffected part with the scale card.
4. Docket pulled. Who took it, which site, what date, what service, what was charged.
5. Intake record read. Pre-existing damage, worn areas, previous repairs, stains and class, pockets, belt.
6. Intake photographs compared, like part with like part.
7. Care label as recorded at intake, checked against the label as it reads today.
8. Route sheet pulled. Method, special instructions, anything annotated on the query rail.
9. Machine log pulled. Machine, programme, load, temperature, extract, drying, operator, time.
10. Any MSDC-F02 authorisation pulled. Exists? Signed before acceptance? Names this failure mode?
11. Garment examined flat, in daylight, in the SOP 01 clause 4.2 order.
12. Failure named in textile terms — colour, dimensional, surface, structural, transfer, component.
13. Comparison against an unaffected part of the same garment completed and photographed.
14. The six questions at clause 6 answered, in order, in writing.
15. Outcome selected: defend, deny, settle or goodwill.
16. Where settling: Table I life expectancy, age band, condition, Table II percentage, arithmetic shown, cleaning charge waived, cap applied if a post-wedding gown.
17. Authority checked: Sharon to $250; Karen above $250 and for anything in writing; broker at or above $2,500.
18. Customer contacted with the outcome and a time frame.
19. Letter drafted for Karen where one is going out.
20. Everything filed against the docket under clause 12.

## Appendix B — The fault decision flow

1. Is the garment damaged, or is it simply not what the customer expected? If it is not damaged, the outcome is **goodwill**. Stop here.
2. Read the care label as recorded at intake. Note the circle, the letter, the bars and any cross.
3. Read the machine log. Note the machine, the programme and the actual parameters run.
4. Did what we ran depart from what the label permitted? If yes, the outcome is **settle**. Stop here.
5. Did we depart from the route sheet, or from our own procedure — belt left on, collar not pre-spotted, pressed when told not to, wrong tissue in the box? If yes, the outcome is **settle**. Stop here.
6. Does the machine log show a process error — wrong temperature, wrong extract, wrong programme? If yes, the outcome is **settle**. Stop here.
7. Was the method actually gentler than the label permitted? Record how, precisely, with the parameters.
8. Is there an MSDC-F02, signed before acceptance, naming this failure mode? Record all three answers separately.
9. Is the failure mode a known manufacturing, trim, coating or labelling fault? Name the DLI TABS bulletin by title. Is there a retailer or maker to go back to? If yes, the outcome is **defend**.
10. Does the care label fail to account for a trim or component, contrary to AS/NZS 1957? If so, say so in the letter.
11. Is the damage at the wear points, symmetrical, gradual, and consistent with age, use or sun? Does the comparison at clause 5.4 support that? If yes, the outcome is **deny**, and find something practical to offer.
12. If none of the above resolves it, the outcome is **settle**. The tie does not go to the business.
13. Apply the authority limits at clause 1.1 before anything is said to the customer.

## Appendix C — Phrases that work

*"I'm sorry — that's not what you should have got back from us."*

*"Sharon's in from seven tomorrow. She'll look at it in the morning and ring you before midday, either way."*
*"I can't tell you today what we'll be able to do, but you'll hear from us tomorrow."*

*"What would you like us to do about it?"*
*"Would you rather have the garment put right, or would you rather we sorted it out another way?"*

*"The beads are plastic rather than glass, and the solvent takes the surface off them. It isn't the cloth and it isn't the stitching."*
*"Curtains relax when they're taken down and cleaned — up to about three per cent. Yours have moved one and a half."*

*"This one's ours. I'm not going to dress it up."*
*"We found it in our own machine log."*
*"Our procedure says that shouldn't have gone to perc, and it did."*

*"That's depreciated value rather than the price of a new one, which is how textile claims are settled everywhere in the trade."*
*"The cleaning charge comes off as well; we're not charging you for that."*

**Closing**
*"If you'd rather keep the garment than have the money go the other way, just say — plenty of people would."*
