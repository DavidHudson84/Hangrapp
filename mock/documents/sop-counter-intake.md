## 1. Purpose and scope

This procedure covers everything that happens between a customer putting a garment on the counter and that garment reaching the plant on a route sheet.

It applies at both sites, to every article we accept, every time. Dry cleaning, laundry, wet cleaning, press-only work, alterations, curtains, household textiles and anything we send out to a specialist. It applies to a $12 shirt and to a $3,200 wedding gown in exactly the same way, because the shirt is where the habit is formed.

It binds everyone who stands at a counter: Sharon Delaney, Emma Sutton and Chloe Barnes at Bendigo, Rebecca Toomey at Kangaroo Flat, Dylan Pearce whenever he takes work on the van, and Peter Nguyen, Marco Rossi, Julie Harmon or Anh Tran whenever any of them stands in on the front.

There is no light version for a busy Saturday. About 1,050 garments a week go through this procedure in a normal week and 1,400 a week in the September to November peak — balls, race week and weddings — and the peak is exactly when we get it wrong. Roughly 18% of that volume comes in through Kangaroo Flat, where one person is on the floor alone.

## 2. Why intake decides claims

Almost every claim we lose is lost at the counter, not in the plant.

That is worth sitting with, because it is not how it feels. When a garment comes back damaged, everyone looks at the machine. But the plant does only what the route sheet tells it to do. If the route sheet says perc and the garment could not take perc, the machine did its job perfectly and the mistake was made at the counter, five days earlier, by somebody who did not read a label.

The second half of the argument is about evidence. Six weeks after a garment is collected, a customer stands at the counter and says the shine on the shoulders was not there when she brought it in. Nobody in this business remembers that garment. Sharon does not remember it, Peter does not remember it, and the person who took it in does not remember it either. The only thing in the world that can answer her is what was written on the docket at intake. If the docket says *"Shoulders shiny both sides at intake, sun fade across tops"*, the conversation is two minutes long and costs nothing. If the docket says nothing, we cannot prove she is wrong, and in practice that means we pay.

So the record you make in ninety seconds at the counter is the evidence six weeks later. It is what Sharon reads when she assesses under SOP 02. It is what Karen reads before she signs a settlement. It is what goes to Hargreaves & Locke and on to Meridian Underwriting if the claim is big enough to notify, and it is what Consumer Affairs Victoria reads if a customer goes to them. None of those people ever sees the garment as it arrived. They see your handwriting.

Three rules follow, and they run through everything below.

- **Write it as though you will never be asked about it.** You will not be standing there to explain what you meant by "a bit worn".
- **A garment nobody inspected is a garment we cannot defend.** If we did not look, we cannot afterwards say what was or was not there.
- **The customer is not the enemy of the record.** Inspecting in front of them, out loud, is what makes the record credible. A defect they watched you write down is a defect they will not dispute.

## 3. The greeting and the docket

### 3.1 The greeting

Greet the customer, take the garments across the counter and lay them on the intake bench. Get them out of the bag and off the hanger they arrived on before you write anything. You cannot inspect a garment you are holding in one hand while typing with the other, and everybody who has tried it has missed something.

Say, in your own words but to this effect:

> *"I'll just go through these with you before I book them in."*

That single sentence does three jobs. It buys you the ninety seconds. It tells the customer that inspection is routine rather than suspicion. And it puts them beside you looking at the garment, which is where you want them if there is bad news.

### 3.2 The docket

Every article we accept gets a docket. The docket is raised in the app at the counter, before the customer leaves, and never afterwards from memory. A docket written up at the end of the day is a docket with nothing on it.

**Docket numbering.** Bendigo dockets carry a **B-** prefix. Kangaroo Flat dockets carry a **K-** prefix. The number is issued by the app in sequence at the site where the garment is taken, and it is never handwritten, never reused and never edited once the customer has left the counter.

A garment taken at Kangaroo Flat keeps its **K-** number all the way through the Bendigo plant and back again. It is not re-docketed on arrival, and the K- number is what appears on every subsequent record — the route sheet, the machine log, any authorisation, any letter and any claim. Docket K-08812 and docket B-24118 are both live jobs in the same plant on the same day, and the prefix is how anybody assessing a complaint six weeks later knows which counter took it and who to ask.

Individual pieces within a docket are identified as docket number, oblique, piece number:

    B-24118/1   jacket
    B-24118/2   trousers
    B-24118/3   self-belt

That is what goes on the garment tag and on the route sheet, and it is what you quote on the phone.

**Captured on every docket, without exception:**

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Customer name | Surname and given name, spelt back to the customer |
| Phone | Mobile preferred; landline accepted; see clause 3.3 |
| Email | Optional, but ask — it is how letters and quotes go out |
| Site | Set automatically by the terminal; never overridden |
| Staff member | The person who **inspected**, not the person who took payment |
| Pieces | Counted twice, out loud, in front of the customer |
| Article description | Type, colour, brand if there is a maker's label |
| Service | Dry clean, launder, wet clean, press only, alteration, send-out |
| Condition notes | Everything under clause 5 |
| At-risk flag | Set if anything in clause 7 applies |
| Authorisation number | Where an MSDC-F02 has been signed |
| Photographs | Attached before the customer leaves; clause 10 |
| Collection date | A date, not "later in the week" |

**Counting pieces.** Count them twice, out loud, in front of the customer, and make the customer agree the number before you write it. A two-piece suit is two pieces. A three-piece suit is three. A dress with a separate belt is one piece plus a belt, and the belt is written on the docket as its own line so that it cannot be quietly lost between the counter and the conveyor. Curtains are counted in pairs and the number of pairs is written in words as well as figures — *"Four (4) pairs, lined"* — because a figure altered from 4 to 1 is the sort of argument nobody wins.

**Collection date.** Give a date. Standard turnaround is five working days at both sites. Anything going out to a specialist — leather, suede, some wedding gowns, some household textiles — is three to four weeks, and you say that at the counter, at the moment of intake, and not in an apologetic phone call on day six. Kangaroo Flat work goes to Bendigo on Dylan's morning run and comes back on his afternoon run, so a K- docket raised after 10:00 loses a day against the same job taken at Bendigo. Add the day rather than explain it later.

Shirts booked before 09:00 at Bendigo are next-day. Alterations are quoted by Anh and the collection date is not promised until she has seen the garment; write *"date to be confirmed by Anh — customer to be rung"* and then make sure somebody rings.

### 3.3 The customer who will not give a phone number

Some people will not give a number. Some have their reasons and none of them are your business. Do not argue with them and do not make it a scene.

**For ordinary work.** Take the name and the collection date, write **"no contact number given"** on the docket in those words, and say plainly:

> *"That's fine — but it does mean if anything comes up we can't reach you, so it'll just sit here until you come in."*

Then book it in. The note on the docket is what protects us if the garment sits on the conveyor for four weeks.

**For anything at-risk under clause 7.** The rule is **no number, no garment.** We cannot accept a garment we may need to ring about. Say:

> *"With this one I do need a number, because there's a fair chance I'll need to talk to you before we go ahead. If you'd rather not leave one, I'd rather not take the piece."*

If they still refuse, decline the garment under clause 11 and note the decline in the app. This is not negotiable and it is not a matter of judgement. The whole architecture of an at-risk garment is that we stop and ring before we do anything irreversible, and a garment we cannot ring about is a garment we cannot stop on.

## 4. The inspection

### 4.1 Set-up

Stand square to the intake bench with the garment flat in front of you, not draped over your arm and not held up in the air. Flat, on the bench, with room to turn it over.

**Light.** At Bendigo, use the daylight lamp on the counter arm — the 6,500 K bar over the intake bench, switched on, not the ceiling fluoros. At Kangaroo Flat, use the handheld daylight torch kept in the counter drawer, and check the batteries on Monday morning. Ordinary shop lighting is warm and flattering, and it hides yellowing, tide lines and colour loss. Those are the three things customers come back about. Under warm light a jacket with a lighter collar looks fine; under the daylight bar it does not.

Turn the garment over. Look at the inside. Half the defects in a jacket are in the lining and nobody ever finds them by looking at the front.

### 4.2 The order

Work the same order on every garment, every time. Order is not fussiness — order is what stops you skipping the lining because you were talking to the customer about the weather.

1. **Collar and neckline.** Build-up on the inside fold, perspiration marks, a stretched or twisted collar, fraying on the outer edge, worn interfacing. Look at the fold in daylight, and run a thumb along the inside. Product build-up on a collar is the single most common cause of a tide line after cleaning, and it is invisible until the solvent has oxidised it. See clause 7 — a collar with build-up is an at-risk garment and does not go to perc untreated.
2. **Shoulders.** Sun fading across the tops, water marking, shine from a seat back or a bag strap, loose or broken padding, distortion in the sleeve head. Compare left with right and both with the body of the garment. On a jacket, compare the jacket with the trousers.
3. **Sleeves and cuffs.** Worn cuff edges, ink inside the right cuff, missing or mismatched cuff buttons, split seams under the arm, deodorant crystallisation in the underarm, pilling on the forearm.
4. **Front.** Food, drink and oil marks, buttons present and matching, lapel roll and shape, pilling, pulled threads, any repair.
5. **Back.** Chair shine on a jacket, rub marks, the strip across the middle where a bag or a seatbelt sits, vent condition.
6. **Hem.** Dropped hem, previous re-hem, dirt line along the bottom edge, curtain hem weights present and accounted for, kick pleat.
7. **Lining.** The lining is what tears, not the shell. Splits under the arms, at the vent and at the sleeve head. Shattered or perished lining on an older jacket, which will not survive a cycle and is a decline under clause 11.
8. **Pockets.** Every pocket, on every piece, by hand, inside and out. Clause 5.4.
9. **Closures.** Zip runs both ways and is not missing a tooth, hooks and eyes present, press studs closed, buttons counted, buckles and D-rings sound.
10. **Trims.** Beading, sequins, appliqué, contrast panels, leather or suede trim, buckles, shoulder pads, belts, sashes, faux fur collars. Anything attached to the garment that is not the main fabric, because the trim is what fails.

### 4.3 How long it should take

| Article | Target |
|---|---|
| Shirt, laundered | 20 seconds |
| Trousers, skirt, plain dress | 60 seconds |
| Jacket, coat, suit | 90 seconds |
| Anything at-risk under clause 7 | 3 to 5 minutes, including photographs |
| Curtains, per pair | 3 minutes, including the measurement |

Ninety seconds is the number to remember. It is not long. It is about the time it takes to say hello, lay a jacket out, turn it over once, put a hand in four pockets and type six words.

If the queue is four deep, you do not shorten the inspection. You say:

> *"I'll have you booked in in about two minutes — this one needs a proper look."*

Nobody has ever left over two minutes. Two minutes of queue is cheaper than a $476 settlement, and considerably cheaper than the afternoon Karen spends on a letter.

## 5. What must be recorded

Everything found at clause 4 that is not perfect goes on the docket. Write it so that it means the same thing to somebody reading it in six weeks who has never seen the garment and cannot ask you what you meant.

The rule for every note is three things: **where, what, how big.**

Good: *"Split in lining under left arm, approx 60 mm."*
Useless: *"Bit of damage."*

### 5.1 Pre-existing damage

Not "some damage" and not "used condition". Name the part of the garment, name the defect, give a size in millimetres.

- *"Tear in right rear vent, 15 mm, previously stitched with non-matching thread."*
- *"Two pulled threads, front left panel, approx 40 mm apart."*
- *"Burn or scorch mark, left cuff, approx 10 mm, edges hardened."*

### 5.2 Missing buttons and closures

Count them and record the count, not the absence.

- *"Four cuff buttons on left sleeve, three on right — one missing at intake."*
- *"Zip runs but is missing the pull tab."*
- *"Two hooks at waistband, one bent."*

If a button is loose, remove it at the counter, put it in the docket envelope, and note *"one loose front button removed to envelope"*. A button that comes off in the drum is a button that becomes our fault.

### 5.3 Worn areas and previous repairs

Worn areas are where the fabric has already gone and where cleaning will finish it.

- *"Finish worn through at both cuffs and along the collar fold."*
- *"Elbows shiny, both sleeves."*
- *"Right sleeve two shades lighter than the body, consistent with sun."*

Previous repairs are what the customer will later say we did.

- *"Right shoulder seam re-stitched before intake, thread does not match."*
- *"Hem taken up previously, original crease line visible."*
- *"Pocket bag replaced, different fabric."*

A repair we did not note becomes a repair the customer says we caused. That is not cynicism; it is what people honestly remember.

### 5.4 The contents of pockets

Every pocket, on every piece, by hand, inside and out. Breast pockets, ticket pockets, inside pockets, coin pockets, back pockets on trousers, the pocket in the lining nobody uses.

Anything found goes into the docket envelope, is written on the docket, and is handed back at collection. That includes money, and *especially* includes money — a $20 note handed back at collection buys more goodwill than any amount of advertising, and a $20 note that quietly disappears costs a customer for life.

The words **"pockets emptied"** appear on every docket. If nothing was in them, write *"Pockets emptied — nil."* Nil is a finding. A docket with no pocket note is a docket where nobody knows whether the pockets were checked.

Why it matters at this level of insistence: a lip balm through a perc load marks every garment in the drum, and a load is thirty or forty pieces belonging to a dozen different customers. In 2024 one lip balm cost us $780 and eleven apologies. A tissue disintegrates and lints an entire load of dark suiting. A pen is worse. A lighter is a safety incident.

### 5.5 Existing stains and their likely class

Record where it is, how big it is, what colour it is, and what class you think it is. You are not being asked to be certain. You are being asked to say what you saw, so that Peter knows what he is looking at before he starts and so that the record shows the mark was there on the way in.

| Class | What it looks like | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| Tannin / water-based | Tea, coffee, wine, fruit juice — brown to pink, edges usually defined | "Tannin-type mark, front right at waist, approx 30 mm" |
| Oil-based | Grease, make-up, salad oil, engine oil — darker than the fabric, no ring, feels slightly slick | "Oily mark, left lapel, approx 20 mm" |
| Protein | Blood, milk, egg, perspiration — stiff to the touch, dulls and sets with heat | "Protein-type mark, inside collar, approx 15 mm" |
| Dye | Ink, grass, some cosmetics, transferred dye — strongly coloured, sharp edge | "Blue ink, inside right cuff, approx 15 mm" |
| Invisible / aged | Sugar, soft drink, white wine, perspiration — invisible now, browns after heat | "Suspected invisible sugar staining, front skirt panel" |
| Combination | Most food marks — an oily component and a water-based one | "Food mark, front, approx 25 mm, oily and tannin" |
| Unknown | You genuinely cannot tell | "Stiffened area lower back, approx 150 mm across, origin unknown" |

The invisible class is the one that catches people. A clear drink spilled at a wedding leaves nothing to see. Six months later, after the heat of a press, it is a brown patch, and the customer is certain we made it. Whenever a garment has been to a function and has been sitting since, ask, and write the answer down:

> *"Has it had a drink on it at any point? It doesn't have to show — sugar goes brown when it's heated, so it's better if I note it now."*

**Never promise removal of any mark. Ever.** Not "we'll get that out", not "that'll come out fine", not a confident nod. What you say is:

> *"I'll have a go at that one, but I can't promise it — it's been in a while."*

And then write on the docket what you told the customer: *"Advised mark may not lift."*

### 5.6 Measurements

Curtains, drapes, tablecloths and anything else where length matters are **measured at intake, and the measurement is written on the docket**, in millimetres, taken from the top of the heading tape to the bottom of the hem, with the customer watching you do it.

Relaxation shrinkage of up to 3% is normal and inherent in a hung cotton or linen curtain and is not a cleaning fault, but you can only say that if you know what the drop was on the way in. One pair measured at 2,650 mm at intake and 2,610 mm on return is a 1.5% change and a conversation you win. The same pair with no intake measurement is a conversation you lose.

Household textile work also requires Form MSDC-F04, the drapery advice, signed at intake. It sets out shrinkage, sun rot, lining separation and the fact that curtains often hang differently after cleaning until they settle.

## 6. The care label

### 6.1 Finding it and reading it

Find the label before you decide the method. It is normally in the left side seam about hip height, at the neck, in the waistband, or inside a pocket bag. On trousers, check the waistband and the left side seam. On curtains, check the top corner behind the heading tape, where there usually is not one.

Read it. Then read it out to the customer if it says anything unusual, because that is the moment to have the conversation rather than after the fact.

Care labelling in Australia is governed by **AS/NZS 1957 Textiles — Care labelling**. The standard requires the label to specify a care method that is suitable for the garment **as a whole**, including its trim, its lining, its interfacing and its components. That last point is the one that matters at our counter. A gown labelled with a plain "P" but carrying dense plastic bugle beading has a label that does not account for the trim, and that is non-compliance with AS/NZS 1957. It does not make the garment safe to clean, and it does not make us liable when the trim fails — but it does mean the label cannot be relied on, so the garment is at-risk under clause 7 no matter how reassuring the symbol looks.

When that is the case, write it on the docket in those words: *"Care label does not account for beaded trim — non-compliant with AS/NZS 1957."*

### 6.2 The symbols

There are five basic shapes. Learn the shapes first and the modifiers second.

| Symbol | What it governs | Detail and modifiers |
|---|---|---|
| **Washtub** | Domestic washing | A number inside gives the maximum wash temperature in °C. Dots may be used instead. One bar beneath = mild / permanent press. Two bars = very mild / delicate. A hand in the tub = hand wash only. Crossed out = do not wash. |
| **Triangle** | Bleaching | Plain triangle = any bleach may be used. Two diagonal lines inside = oxygen (non-chlorine) bleach only. Crossed out = do not bleach. |
| **Square** | Drying | A circle inside the square = tumble drying. One dot = low heat, two dots = normal heat, three dots = high. Crossed circle inside the square = do not tumble dry. A vertical line = line dry. A horizontal line = dry flat. A diagonal line in the top corner = dry in the shade. |
| **Circle** | Professional textile care | The letter inside gives the permitted medium. A bar beneath modifies the process. Crossed out = do not dry clean. |
| **Circle — P** | Perchloroethylene, and every solvent permitted under F | Union XL-800, normal process. A barred **P** means a reduced process — shorter cycle, reduced mechanical action, lower drying temperature. |
| **Circle — F** | Hydrocarbon and similar solvents **only**. Perchloroethylene is **not** permitted. | Realstar HM-450. A barred **F** means reduced process on the same machine. |
| **Circle — W** | Professional wet cleaning | Speed Queen washers on the wet clean programme, with the appropriate bar restriction. |
| **Iron** | Ironing and pressing | One dot = maximum 110 °C, no steam in most cases. Two dots = maximum 150 °C. Three dots = maximum 200 °C. Crossed out = do not iron. Crossed steam lines beneath the iron = do not steam. |

**The three rules that matter at the counter:**

1. **A bar means gentler.** One bar reduced, two bars very gentle. If in doubt, treat one bar as two.
2. **A cross means never.** A crossed circle is not a suggestion. If the garment must be cleaned and the circle is crossed, it goes to at-risk and an authorisation, or it is declined.
3. **F never means P.** A garment labelled F that goes into perc is a claim we cannot defend, cannot insure against, and have already paid once — $640 in 2025, a fused jacket whose fronts delaminated.

### 6.3 When the label is missing, cut out, illegible or wrong

| Situation | What you do |
|---|---|
| No label at all | The garment is at-risk under clause 7. Authorisation required. Record *"no care label present"*. |
| Label cut out | Same as no label. Ask the customer why it was cut out — usually it scratched — and write the answer down. |
| Illegible, faded or washed away | Treat as no label, unless you can read the circle and its modifiers with complete certainty. Half a symbol is no symbol. |
| Label contradicts the fabric | Believe the fabric, not the label. A "machine wash 40" label sewn into a fused wool jacket is wrong, and following it will destroy the garment. Route to the gentler of the two options and note the contradiction on the docket. |
| Two labels that disagree | Follow the more restrictive of the two. |
| Label in a language you cannot read | The symbols are international and are the same in every language. Read the symbols and ignore the words. |
| Retailer's label only, no care label | No care label. At-risk. |

Never guess a method and then write it on the route sheet as though it had come from a label. Write **"no care label — method by inspection"**, say which fibre you believe it to be and why, and let the plant see that a judgement was made rather than a fact recorded.

## 7. At-risk garments

A garment is at-risk if any one of the following is true. This is a list, not a judgement call. If it is on the list, it is at-risk, and clauses 8, 9 and 10 follow automatically.

| At-risk feature | What specifically can go wrong |
|---|---|
| **Beading and sequins** | Plastic beads and sequins cloud, go matt, soften, dissolve or lose their surface coating in solvent. Glass beads generally survive; plastic frequently does not, and no label tells you which you have. The thread holding the beading can also dissolve, dropping beads that were otherwise sound. Bugle beads are worse than round. |
| **Applied prints, transfers, foils, flock** | Lift at the edges, crack across the flex points, go tacky, or transfer onto the panel they are folded against. Heat in finishing finishes off anything the solvent started. Screen prints on knitwear are particularly prone. |
| **PU, PVC and coated fabrics** | The coating separates from its backing, goes sticky, crazes into a fine crackle or lifts in sheets. Both solvent and heat do it, and an older coating will do it without either. Very often labelled as though the coating were not there at all. |
| **Bonded and fused constructions** | Fusible interfacing in collars, cuffs, plackets, front panels and waistbands delaminates, leaving bubbling and rippling that no amount of pressing will remove. Common in cheaper tailoring, in anything over five years old, and in anything stored in a hot roof space. |
| **Leather and suede trim** | Colour transfer from the trim onto a pale shell, stiffening, shrinkage, loss of applied surface finish, and hardening at the edges. A leather trim can bleed into a cream garment in a single cycle. Whole leather garments go out to the specialist; they are never run in-house. |
| **Faux fur and pile fabrics** | Matting, flattening, permanent loss of lustre, and shrinkage of the knitted backing which distorts the whole panel. Very rarely recoverable once matted. |
| **Unlabelled garments** | Unknown fibre, unknown coating, unknown trim, unknown dye stability. Everything else in this table becomes possible and none of it is predictable. |
| **Garments over five years old** | Weakened fibres, perished elastic, shattered silk in linings, degraded interfacing, and oxidised invisible stains that appear as brown marks the first time heat is applied. Age is not a fault, but it removes the margin for error. |
| **Wedding gowns and formalwear** | Multiple substrates in one garment — mikado, tulle, horsehair, boning, glue-set appliqué — each behaving differently. Invisible sugar staining from the reception. Very high replacement values and an emotional weight that makes any conversation harder. |
| **Curtains, drapes and household textiles** | Sun-rotted face fabric that tears in handling, lining that has degraded at a different rate from the face, and relaxation shrinkage of up to 3% which is inherent to the cloth and is not a cleaning fault. Requires Form MSDC-F04 and a measurement under clause 5.6. |
| **Anything already damaged** | Cleaning does not cause the existing damage, but it will complete it. Split seams open further, worn areas go through, loose beading comes off, a cracked coating flakes. The customer will remember the garment as it went in. |
| **Anything the customer says is precious** | Sentimental, irreplaceable, a one-off, "it was my mother's". The value here is not in dollars and cannot be settled with money, which makes the conversation before cleaning more important, not less. |
| **Self-belts, sashes and detachable trims** | A dyed self-belt left attached crocks and bleeds onto the garment in the drum, and the belt buckle abrades the face fabric. **Every belt, sash and detachable trim comes off at intake, is docketed as its own piece, and is cleaned separately or not at all.** Where the care label itself says to remove the belt, quote the label on the docket. |
| **Collars showing product build-up** | Hair product, sunscreen and perspiration residue oxidise in perchloroethylene and leave a tide line across the collar and shoulders that is *lighter* than the surrounding cloth and cannot be put back. **Route to hydrocarbon, or pre-spot on the board first. Never send a built-up collar to perc untreated.** |
| **Pale and unstable dyes** | Deep red, navy and black on silk, and anything hand-dyed, may bleed. Test on an inside seam if there is any doubt, and note the test on the docket. |

## 8. The at-risk conversation

You are not warning the customer off their own garment. You are telling them what we know, before they decide, so the decision is theirs and it is informed. Done well, this conversation is the single strongest piece of customer service we offer, because almost nobody else does it.

### 8.1 How to raise it

Have the conversation **while the garment is on the bench**, pointing at the actual thing you are talking about. Pointing at a bead and saying what a bead does reads as expertise. The same words on the phone a week later read as an excuse.

Lead with the offer to show them, not with the risk:

> *"Before I book this in — can I show you something?"*

Then describe the mechanism, not the disaster. People accept a mechanism.

> *"These beads are plastic rather than glass. Plastic beads sometimes go cloudy in cleaning, because the solvent takes the surface off them. It isn't the fabric and it isn't the stitching — it's the bead itself, and there's no way to know in advance which ones will do it."*

Then say what we will do about it:

> *"We'd run it in the hydrocarbon machine, which is the gentlest thing we've got — gentler than the label actually asks for — in a net bag, cool, and on its own rather than in a load."*

Then be honest about the limit of that:

> *"That's the best anyone can do with it. I still can't promise the beads won't change."*

And then leave the decision with them:

> *"I'd rather tell you now than ring you in a week. It's your call."*

### 8.2 Words that work

- *"I'd rather tell you now than ring you in a week."*
- *"If it were mine I'd still clean it — but I'd want to know first."*
- *"The garment's fine. It's the trim I'm not certain about."*
- *"Nine times out of ten this is fine. I don't know which one this is."*
- *"We'll use the gentlest method we've got and I'll have the plant run it on its own."*
- *"This is what the form is for — it's not us washing our hands, it's a record that I told you."*

### 8.3 Words that must not be used

- **"It'll be fine."** It might not be, and you have just given a guarantee.
- **"We can't be held responsible."** Untrue as a matter of law, and it sounds like a shield going up.
- **"You'd be mad to clean this."** You have insulted the garment and the person who bought it.
- **"That's your risk, not ours."** Same problem, said faster.
- **"We do this all the time."** Then why are we having the conversation?

### 8.4 When the customer pushes back

Some customers will say another cleaner never mentioned any of this. Do not argue about the other cleaner and do not imply they were careless.

> *"That may well be, and it may well have been perfectly fine. I'm telling you what I can see today."*

Some will ask for a guarantee.

> *"I can guarantee the method and the care we take. What I can't guarantee is how a five-year-old coating behaves in solvent — nobody can. That's exactly what the form is for."*

Some will get annoyed and say we are just covering ourselves. The honest answer is yes, partly, and it is also the only way they get to make the choice.

> *"You're right that it protects us. It also means you're deciding, rather than finding out afterwards."*

Some will say just do it and they will take their chances — and then refuse to sign. That is a decline under clause 11. A verbal acceptance of risk is worth nothing and is not what this procedure allows.

### 8.5 The standing rule

**We would rather decline a garment than take one we cannot clean safely.**

Nobody at this business has ever been in trouble for turning work away. Several people have been in trouble for taking it. If you are standing at the counter genuinely unsure, the answer is not to take it and hope — it is to ring Sharon, or to decline it politely and keep the customer.

## 9. The at-risk authorisation

### 9.1 When it is mandatory

Form MSDC-F02, *Authorisation to Proceed — Garment Accepted at Customer's Risk*, is mandatory:

- for every garment falling under any line of clause 7, at both sites, without exception;
- whenever the customer's stated value of a single article exceeds $500;
- whenever a garment is going to an outside specialist;
- whenever the customer has told you the item is irreplaceable or sentimental;
- whenever the care label is absent, cut out or illegible; and
- whenever you have advised the customer that a mark may not lift and they want it attempted anyway.

If you are wondering whether it is mandatory, it is mandatory.

### 9.2 How it is completed

The form is generated from the docket in the app and printed at the counter, so that the customer, the docket number and the garment description are already correct and cannot be transcribed wrongly. Its blocks are set out in full at Appendix B.

Two parts are filled by hand and both matter.

**Condition at intake** is a numbered list of every pre-existing defect you found at clause 4. It should be specific and unflattering. This list is the whole point of the form. *"Finish worn through at both cuffs and along the collar fold. Right sleeve two shades lighter than the body. Stiffened area approximately 150 mm across the lower back where something has dried into the skin."* That is a useful list. *"Some wear"* is not.

**The risks** must be the risks of *this* garment, in plain words, numbered so they can be pointed at. *"Colour will change and may not return to the original shade. Further loss of finish at the cuffs and collar is likely. The mark on the lower back may not come out at all. The quilted panels may not take the re-finish evenly. Turnaround is three to four weeks, not the usual five days."*

**"Damage may occur" is not a risk statement.** It tells the customer nothing, it tells an assessor nothing, and it will not help anybody six weeks later.

Where a DLI TABS bulletin covers the failure mode, name it by title on the form and summarise its position in two or three sentences. For a leather garment that is *DLI TABS — Leather, Suede, Velvet & Napped Fabrics*; for beading and applied trim it is *DLI TABS — Prints, Embellishments, Trims & Notions*.

### 9.3 Signed before the garment is accepted, never after

The customer signs before the garment is accepted. Not at collection. Not when it comes back from the plant. Not when Peter has rung to say something has gone wrong.

**An authorisation signed after the event is worth nothing.** It is not a defence at law, it will not be accepted by the insurer, and producing one contaminates every honest record we hold. We have been here: in 2022 we paid $1,150 on a beaded jacket because the form was signed at collection instead of at intake, and the signature made the position worse rather than better.

The form does not exclude the consumer guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law that cannot be excluded, and it says so in those words on its face. What it does is record that a specific risk was explained to a specific customer on a specific day, and that they chose to proceed. That is all it does. It is also, in practice, enough — see SOP 02 clause 6.

### 9.4 Where it is filed

The signed original is scanned into the app against the docket **before the garment leaves the counter**, so that the plant and the office can see it without going to look for paper. The paper original goes into the intake file at the site where it was signed, filed by docket number.

Kangaroo Flat originals travel to Bendigo with the garment on Dylan's run and are filed at Bendigo. A copy is offered to the customer every time, and most take it.

Authorisations are retained for seven years under SOP 02 clause 12, whether or not anything ever goes wrong.

## 10. Photographs

### 10.1 When they are mandatory

Photographs are mandatory for:

- every at-risk garment under clause 7;
- every garment taken on a signed MSDC-F02;
- every curtain and household textile job;
- every garment where the customer has raised a concern at the counter;
- every garment being sent out to a specialist; and
- every garment declined under clause 11, before it goes back over the counter.

They are taken on the site tablet, into the app, against the docket. Never on a personal phone, for the plain reason that a photograph on somebody's phone is not a business record and cannot be produced.

### 10.2 How many, and what must be in frame

**Minimum six.** For a leather jacket, a wedding gown or a set of curtains, expect eight to ten. There is no maximum and nobody has ever complained about too many.

1. The whole garment, front, flat on the bench, filling the frame.
2. The whole garment, back, flat on the bench.
3. The care label, close enough that every symbol can be read on screen.
4. Each area of pre-existing damage, close.
5. Each at-risk feature — the beading, the coating, the trim, the print, the worn cuff.
6. The docket number in frame with the garment in at least one shot, so the set cannot be attached to the wrong job.

**The scale reference.** The yellow 150 mm scale card lives on the counter at both sites and goes in frame beside anything photographed close. A mark photographed without a scale is a mark of unknown size, and "approximately this big" is not a measurement. Lay the card flat beside the mark, not on top of it.

Shoot in the daylight lamp or torch, not the ceiling light, and do not use flash on anything shiny — flash on beading photographs as a white blur and shows nothing.

### 10.3 Where they are stored

Photographs attach to the docket in the app and are visible to the plant, the office and the owner. They are retained for seven years under SOP 02 clause 12. They are never deleted, edited, filtered or cropped after the fact — a cropped photograph is worth less than no photograph, because it invites the question of what was cropped out.

## 11. Declining a garment

We decline in four situations, and only four.

1. **We cannot identify a safe method.** No label, an unidentifiable fibre or coating, and the customer will not sign an authorisation. If we do not know what it is and nobody will accept the risk, we cannot proceed.
2. **The garment will not survive the process.** Perished elastic, shattered silk, rotted lining, a coating already crazed and lifting, a seam holding by two threads, sun-rotted curtain fabric that tears in the hand. Cleaning will not have caused it, but cleaning will complete it, and the customer will remember only that they gave it to us intact.
3. **The soiling is something we are not equipped to handle safely.** Fuel or solvent contamination, sewage, heavy mould growth through the substrate, sharps in the seams, anything recovered from a fire scene. These are health and plant-safety questions, not textile ones.
4. **The expectation cannot be met.** The customer wants a guarantee we cannot give, or wants a set stain removed and has said they will accept nothing less than complete removal.

### How to say it

Short, warm, honest about the reason, and always with somewhere for them to go next.

> *"I'm not going to take this one, and I'll tell you why. The coating on the shoulders has already started to lift, and cleaning will lift the rest of it. I don't want to hand you back something worse than what you gave me. What I'd suggest is ..."*

Then suggest something real: a specialist we know, an alteration Anh can do, a re-line, airing and brushing rather than cleaning, or simply leaving it alone until it has to be cleaned. A customer who leaves with advice and no invoice comes back. A customer who leaves with a ruined garment does not.

Record the decline and the reason in the app against the customer, and photograph the garment before it goes back over the counter. If they take it to the other site next week, we need to know what was said this week.

## 12. Handover to the plant

### 12.1 Tagging

Every piece is tagged before it leaves the counter with its docket-and-piece number — B-24118/1 — on the standard tag. Pin at the neck, the waistband or an inside seam. Never through the face fabric of a delicate garment, never through beading, never through a coating, and never through a print.

Coloured tabs on the tag set the route at a glance:

| Tab | Route |
|---|---|
| No tab | Perchloroethylene, Union XL-800, standard process |
| Green | Hydrocarbon, Realstar HM-450 |
| Blue | Wet clean, Speed Queen |
| **Red** | **At-risk. Do not run without reading the authorisation.** |
| White | Send-out to an outside specialist |

A red tab always travels with a green tab or a blue tab as well, because at-risk is a condition, not a method.

### 12.2 The route sheet

The route sheet prints per docket and travels with the garments to the plant. A plant operator must be able to see, without asking anybody and without going back to the counter:

- the docket number and every piece number;
- the article, colour and brand;
- the care label exactly as read at intake, including bars and crosses;
- the method chosen and the machine;
- every pre-existing defect recorded at clause 5;
- every stain, its location, its size and its likely class;
- whether an authorisation exists and its number;
- whether photographs exist; and
- any special instruction — *belt removed and cleaned separately*, *do not press*, *run alone*, *hand finish*, *no steam on trim*, *collar pre-spot before load*.

**If the route sheet does not say it, the plant does not know it.** Peter is not a mind reader and Marco is finishing forty pieces an hour. Anything you decided at the counter that changes what the plant does must be written where the plant will read it.

### 12.3 If the plant disagrees with the intake decision

Peter, Marco or Julie may look at a garment and disagree with the method, the risk assessment or the promise made at the counter. That is the point of having them look, and it is not a criticism of the counter.

1. The operator **stops** and does not run the garment.
2. The garment goes on the **query rail** with the route sheet annotated — what they think and why, in one line, with their initials and the date.
3. The operator tells Sharon the same day. Sharon rules on the method.
4. If the method changes in a way the customer would care about — a longer turnaround, a different machine, a risk that was not discussed — **the customer is rung before anything is run**, and a fresh MSDC-F02 is raised if the new risk is not covered by the one on file.
5. If Sharon and the operator do not agree, Karen decides. Nobody runs the garment in the meantime.
6. The outcome is written back onto the route sheet and into the docket, so the record shows the query and its resolution.

A garment sitting on the query rail overnight becomes a phone call to the customer about the collection date. It does not become a silence.

Kangaroo Flat is a 24 m² drop store in Lansell Square: a counter, racking, a tablet and a hand basin. No plant, no machine, no solvent, no second opinion and nobody else on the floor. Rebecca works alone, and on a Saturday morning the centre is busy.

Everything in this procedure applies at Kangaroo Flat exactly as it applies at Bendigo. In addition:

## 14. Common failures

Six intake mistakes that have actually cost this business money. Every one of them is in this procedure somewhere, and every one of them was a step somebody skipped because it was busy.

| # | What happened | What it cost | The correct action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A dyed self-belt was left attached to a dress. In the drum the belt crocked onto the skirt and left colour transfer across the front. | $310 settlement | Belt removed at intake, docketed as its own piece, cleaned separately or not at all (clause 7). |
| 2 | Collar build-up was not noticed and not noted. The jacket went to perc untreated, the residue oxidised, and the collar and shoulders came back lighter than the body. | $420 settlement | Build-up found at clause 4.2 step 1, recorded, and the garment routed to hydrocarbon or pre-spotted on the board (clause 7). |
| 3 | Pockets were not emptied. A lip balm went through a perc load and marked eleven garments belonging to nine customers. | $780 and eleven apologies | Every pocket emptied by hand and *"pockets emptied"* written on the docket, nil included (clause 5.4). |
| 4 | The care label was not read. A fused jacket labelled **F** was run in perc and the front panels delaminated. | $640 settlement | The circle and its letter read and recorded, and F never routed to perc (clause 6.2). |
| 5 | Four pairs of curtains were accepted with no drop measurement and no Form MSDC-F04. The customer complained of shrinkage and we had nothing to compare against. | Three weeks of argument and a free re-hem | Drop measured in millimetres at intake with the customer watching, written on the docket, and the drapery advice signed (clause 5.6). |
| 6 | An at-risk authorisation on a beaded jacket was signed at collection rather than at intake, after the beading had already dulled. | $1,150 settlement | Signed before the garment was accepted, or the garment declined (clause 9.3). |

Five of the six were a line that was not written on a docket. None of the six was a machine fault. That is the whole argument of clause 2, in a table.

## Appendix A — Intake checklist card (Form MSDC-F01)

Laminated, on the counter at both sites, beside the terminal.

1. Garments out of the bag and flat on the bench.
2. Daylight lamp or torch on.
3. Count the pieces out loud, twice, with the customer.
4. Find the care label. Read the circle, the bars and the crosses.
5. Work the order: collar, shoulders, cuffs, front, back, hem, lining, pockets, closures, trims.
6. Empty every pocket by hand. Write "pockets emptied" even when nil.
7. Remove every belt, sash and detachable trim. Docket it separately.
8. Write every defect: where, what, how big.
9. Write every stain: where, size, colour, likely class. Promise nothing.
10. Measure anything where length matters.
11. At-risk under clause 7? Talk it through, photograph, authorise, red tab.
12. Confirm the collection date and the phone number.
13. Tag each piece, print the route sheet, rack it.

If you are unsure at any point: stop, and ring Sharon on (03) 5443 8817.

## Appendix B — Authorisation to proceed (Form MSDC-F02)

The form prints with the following blocks, in this order.

**Heading line:** AUTHORISATION TO PROCEED — GARMENT ACCEPTED AT CUSTOMER'S RISK

1. **Identification.** Customer name. Docket number. Site. Date. Staff member.
2. **The garment.** Full description — type, brand, colour, style, construction — and the customer's stated value.
3. **The care label.** Quoted exactly as it reads. Whether it is present, absent, illegible or cut out. Any non-compliance with AS/NZS 1957 noted, with the reason.
4. **Condition at intake.** A numbered list of every pre-existing defect found at clause 4. Specific and unflattering. This list is the whole point of the form.
5. **What we propose to do.** The method, the machine or the specialist, and whether treatment is whole-garment or localised.
6. **The risks.** A numbered list, specific to this garment, in plain words, including the turnaround where it differs from the standard five days.
7. **The industry position.** Where a DLI TABS bulletin covers the failure mode, it is named by title and its position summarised in two or three sentences.
8. **Authorisation wording.** That the customer has read the above; that they accept the garment is accepted at their risk; that they authorise us to proceed; and that this does not exclude the guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law that cannot be excluded.
9. **Signatures.** Customer signature, printed name and date. Staff signature, printed name and date.

## Appendix C — Care symbol reference (Form MSDC-F03)

**Washtub — domestic washing.** The number inside is the maximum temperature in °C. One bar beneath means a mild process, two bars a very mild process. A hand in the tub means hand wash only. Crossed out means do not wash.

**Triangle — bleaching.** Plain means any bleach may be used. Two diagonal lines inside mean oxygen (non-chlorine) bleach only. Crossed out means do not bleach.

**Square — drying.** A circle inside means tumble drying: one dot low heat, two dots normal, three dots high. A crossed circle inside means do not tumble dry. A vertical line means line dry, a horizontal line means dry flat, and a diagonal line in the top corner means dry in the shade.

**Circle — professional textile care.** **P** means perchloroethylene and every solvent permitted under F — the Union XL-800. **F** means hydrocarbon only and perc is not permitted — the Realstar HM-450. **W** means professional wet cleaning. One bar beneath means a mild process, two bars a very mild process, and a crossed circle means do not dry clean.

**Iron — ironing and pressing.** One dot is a maximum of 110 °C, two dots 150 °C, three dots 200 °C. Crossed out means do not iron. Crossed steam lines beneath the iron mean do not steam.

**Remember three things:** a bar means gentler, a cross means never, and F never means P.
