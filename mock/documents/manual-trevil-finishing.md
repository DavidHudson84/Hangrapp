## 1. Front matter

### 1.1 Scope of this manual

This manual covers the three Trevil finishing machines supplied as a finishing
line: the Diamant SA-90 steam-air cabinet finisher, the Princess FF-300 form
finisher, and the Spotmaster SB-40 spotting board. The three machines are
described in one manual because they share a steam supply, a compressed air
supply and an operator, and because a garment normally passes through the
spotting board before it reaches either finisher.

The manual is written for three readers:

- the **operator**, who loads and runs the machines and does the daily and
  weekly maintenance;
- the **spotter**, who works at the SB-40 and makes decisions about chemistry
  and about which garments must not be treated;
- the **technician**, who holds the relevant electrical, plumbing or mechanical
  licence and does the work marked TECHNICIAN in this manual.

The manual does not teach finishing and it does not teach garment
identification. It assumes the reader is a competent presser or spotter. It
tells you what each machine does, what it needs from the building, what to set,
what to check, and what to do when it stops.

Keep this manual in the finishing room, not in the office. If a machine is
sold, relocated or transferred, this manual and the commissioning record in
Appendix A go with it.

Nothing in this manual overrides a legal duty imposed on the occupier of the
premises. Where a requirement of this manual conflicts with a requirement of a
regulator, the regulator's requirement applies.

### 1.2 How to use this manual

- **If you are new to the finishing room**, read Section 2 (Safety) in full
  before you touch any of the three machines. Then read the description section
  for the machine you have been put on: Section 5 for the SA-90, Section 6 for
  the FF-300, Section 7 for the SB-40.
- **If you are setting a programme**, the parameter list and the programme table
  for the SA-90 are at 5.5 and 5.6, and for the FF-300 at 6.5 and 6.6. Print the
  programme table and keep it at the machine.
- **If you are spotting**, Section 7 is the whole of your reference. The stain
  treatment table is at 7.6. Read 7.8 (fibre cautions) and 7.9 (when not to
  spot) before you use the table, not after.
- **If a machine has stopped and is showing a code**, go straight to Section 9.
  Find the code. The table tells you whether an operator may clear it or whether
  a technician is required. If the table says technician, stop and call one.
- **If you are ordering parts**, use Section 10 and quote the serial number of
  the machine with every order.
- **If a contractor is quoting service work**, Section 8 sets out the intervals
  and Section 11 sets out what voids warranty.

Cross-references are written as "see 7.6" meaning sub-section 7.6. Fault codes
are always written in the form SA-04, FF-03 or SB-05 and mean the same thing
wherever they appear in this manual. The two-letter prefix identifies the
machine: SA is the Diamant SA-90, FF is the Princess FF-300, SB is the
Spotmaster SB-40.

### 1.4 Machine identification

Each machine carries a data plate:

- **SA-90** — right-hand side panel, 1,450 mm above floor level, beside the
  electrical isolator. Carries model, serial number, year of manufacture, supply
  voltage, full load current, total connected load, steam design pressure and
  maximum air temperature.
- **FF-300** — rear panel above the steam inlet, 1,300 mm above floor level.
  Carries the same data plus the maximum form inflation pressure.
- **SB-40** — underside of the board frame at the left-hand front leg. Carries
  model, serial number, year of manufacture, supply voltage, vacuum motor rating
  and the maximum regulated pressure at the chemical guns.

Do not paint over, cover or remove a data plate. If a plate becomes unreadable,
order a replacement quoting the serial number recorded at the head of this
manual.

### 1.5 Intended use

The SA-90 and the FF-300 are intended to finish clean, dry or nearly dry textile
garments by the controlled application of saturated steam followed by heated air
and a cooling airflow. The SB-40 is intended for the localised removal of stains
from textile garments using steam, compressed air, vacuum extraction and the
proprietary spotting chemistry listed at 7.4.

None of the three machines may be used:

- on a garment that is wet with solvent, or that has come directly out of a dry
  cleaning machine without completing its drying and deodorising cycle;
- on a garment contaminated with flammable liquid, oxidiser, paint solvent,
  adhesive or unidentified chemical residue;
- to dry a garment that has been washed and not extracted;
- as a bench, a shelf, or a place to hang finished work.

The SB-40 must not be used with any chemistry other than that listed at 7.4, or
with any product for which the Safety Data Sheet is not held on site.

### 1.6 Residual risks

Even when all three machines are used as intended and all guards are fitted, the
following risks remain and must be managed by the occupier:

- burns from steam, condensate and hot metal at all three machines;
- inhalation of steam and of chemical mist at the spotting board;
- eye injury from the air gun, from the chemical guns, and from splashback off
  the board;
- skin contact with acidic, alkaline and solvent spotting agents;
- fire risk from volatile dry-side spotting agents, in particular amyl acetate;
- slips on a wet floor around the spotting board;
- manual handling of heavy coats onto the SA-90 hanger and of the FF-300 form
  cover during replacement;
- electrical risk from equipment operated in a wet area.

## 2. Safety

### 2.1 General

**WARNING.** The finishing room contains steam at up to 690 kPa, surfaces above
150 °C, compressed air at 620 kPa and chemicals that burn skin and eyes. Three
of the four will injure a person without making any noise first. Do not work in
the finishing room without the personal protective equipment listed at 2.6.

Only trained personnel may operate these machines. Training must be recorded and
must cover, as a minimum: the emergency stop locations, the steam isolation
valve locations, the meaning of every fault code for the machine the person will
operate, the PPE requirements, and the location of the eyewash and the Safety
Data Sheets.

### 2.2 Steam and hot surfaces

Steam supplied to these machines is saturated steam at up to 690 kPa. At 550 kPa
the saturation temperature is approximately 160 °C. Steam escaping from a failed
hose or a cracked fitting is invisible for the first 100 mm to 200 mm of its
path and will remove skin at any distance at which it can still be heard.

**DANGER.** Never search for a steam leak with your hand. Use a board, a broom
handle or a sheet of paper held at arm's length and watch for the paper to be
pushed, wetted or cut.

Hot surfaces on these machines include:

- the SA-90 steam manifold, cabinet floor tray and the lower 400 mm of the
  cabinet rear wall;
- the SA-90 hot air duct and heater housing, which remain above 60 °C for up to
  20 minutes after the last cycle;
- the FF-300 steam ring, blower housing and heater housing;
- the SB-40 steam gun body, hose and nozzle, at all times while the steam
  isolation valve is open;
- all condensate pipework and steam traps at all three machines.

**WARNING.** The SB-40 steam gun nozzle reaches approximately 155 °C in normal
use. Do not rest it on the board cover. Return it to its holder after every
application. A gun left lying on the board will scorch the cover and will burn
the next hand that reaches for the board.

Do not open the SA-90 cabinet during the steam phase. Do not stand directly in
front of the FF-300 form during the pre-steam phase; steam vents forward through
the garment opening.

### 2.3 Chemical hazards at the spotting board

The spotting board is the most hazardous position in the finishing room. The
chemistry set listed at 7.4 includes strong alkalis, strong acids, an oxidising
bleach, a reducing agent, a volatile ester and hydrocarbon solvent.

**WARNING.** The following combinations must never be made on the board, in a
bottle, in the drip tray or in a rinse pot:

- oxygen bleach with any acid formula, including tannin formula and rust
  remover — releases oxygen violently and may release chlorine-like irritants
  where residues of other products are present;
- rust remover with any alkaline product — exothermic, and generates a corrosive
  aerosol at the fabric surface;
- amyl acetate with any oxidiser;
- any two products from different bottles decanted into one container "to save a
  step".

Flush the board with steam and vacuum it dry between an acid-side and an
alkali-side treatment. This is not optional and it is not a courtesy to the next
garment; it is how the operator avoids generating a reaction under their own
face.

**WARNING — amyl acetate.** Amyl acetate (TVC-05) is a flammable liquid with a
flash point of approximately 25 °C and a strong pear-drop odour. It must be:

- used only under the local exhaust hood, with the hood running;
- kept in its original 250 mL supplier bottle with the cap closed between uses;
- kept away from the steam gun, the heater of any nearby machine, and any
  ignition source;
- never decanted into a chemical gun bottle;
- never used in a quantity greater than is needed to wet the stain.

The volatile dry-side solvent (TVC-09) is a hydrocarbon with a flash point above
62 °C. It is less hazardous than amyl acetate but must still be used under
exhaust and kept closed.

### 2.4 Ventilation and local exhaust at the spotting board

The SB-40 is fitted with a rear-draught local exhaust hood connected to a
dedicated extraction fan discharging to atmosphere. The hood must achieve a face
velocity of not less than 0.5 m/s measured at the centre of the hood opening,
corresponding to approximately 850 m³/h at this hood size.

**DANGER.** Do not use the chemical guns, amyl acetate or the dry-side solvent
when the extraction fan is not running. There is no safe way to do this for
"just one spot".

The extraction fan must be interlocked with, or started before, the vacuum
blower. A green lamp on the SB-40 control box indicates airflow proved by the
differential pressure switch across the hood. If the lamp is out, the machine
will raise SB-11; stop chemical work and report it.

Face velocity must be measured and recorded quarterly (see 8.5). General
finishing room ventilation must provide not less than 8 air changes per hour
with the finishing room doors closed. Make-up air must be provided; extraction
without make-up air will pull the hood short. Exhaust discharge must not be
located where it can re-enter the building through a door, a window or an air
intake.

The SB-40 hood must **not** be joined into a common duct with the finisher
vents. A common duct allows the finishers' higher-flow branch to short-circuit
the hood.

### 2.5 Compressed air and eye injury

The SB-40 air gun and the SA-90 sleeve expander circuit are supplied with
compressed air at 620 kPa. Compressed air at this pressure will:

- drive lint, fibre, dried chemistry and rust particles into an unprotected eye
  at a speed that no blink reflex will beat;
- inject air under the skin through a cut or a hangnail;
- atomise whatever is lying on the board into a breathable mist.

**DANGER.** Never direct the air gun at a person, at your own hand, or at your
own clothing. Never use the air gun to clean yourself down. Never use it to
sweep the floor or the bench.

**WARNING.** Eye protection is mandatory whenever the air gun or either chemical
gun is used. This applies to the operator and to anyone within 2 m of the board.

### 2.7 Electrical safety near water

The SB-40 sits in a wet area. The board is wetted, the floor around it is
wetted, and the operator's hands are wet.

**DANGER.** The SB-40 supply must be protected by a residual current device of
30 mA rating. Test the RCD monthly using its own test button and record the
result in the weekly log sheet at Appendix B.

- Do not hose or flood-wash the floor while any machine is energised.
- Do not use a damaged flexible lead. Report and tag it out.
- Do not run extension leads across the finishing room floor.
- Keep the SB-40 control box lid closed and latched. It is rated IP54 with the
  lid closed and IP00 with it open.
- Steam and water must never be directed at the control box, at the lamp
  transformer or at the vacuum motor.

**TECHNICIAN.** Any work inside a control enclosure, any change to the supply,
and any RCD replacement is licensed electrical work.

### 2.8 Emergency stop

Each machine has an emergency stop:

- **SA-90** — red mushroom head, front upright, right-hand side, 1,050 mm above
  floor level.
- **FF-300** — red mushroom head, control pedestal front face; and a second
  mushroom head on the rear panel beside the steam inlet.
- **SB-40** — red mushroom head, front rail below the board, left of the vacuum
  pedal.

Pressing an emergency stop de-energises the heaters, the fan or blower, the
tension and inflation drives, and closes the steam solenoid on that machine.
It does not isolate the incoming electrical supply, and it does not isolate the
steam supply upstream of the solenoid. To make a machine safe for maintenance,
use the lock-out procedure at 8.2.

Emergency stops must be tested weekly (see 8.3 to 8.5). A machine whose
emergency stop does not stop the machine must be tagged out of service
immediately.

### 2.9 First aid — steam or contact burn

1. Move the person away from the source. Do not go back for the machine.
2. Cool the burn under cool running water for **20 minutes**. Do not use ice,
   iced water, butter, oil, toothpaste or any cream.
3. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn while cooling, unless
   fabric is stuck to the skin. Do not pull fabric off a burn.
4. Cover loosely with cling film laid along the limb, or a clean non-fluffy
   cloth. Do not wrap tightly and do not wrap circumferentially.
5. Keep the person warm elsewhere on the body; 20 minutes of cooling will chill
   them.
6. Seek medical attention for any burn that is larger than a 20-cent coin, any
   burn to the face, hands, feet, groin or across a joint, any burn that is
   white, brown or leathery, and any burn to a young or an older worker.
7. Record the incident. A steam burn that needed more than a plaster is a
   notifiable-incident question; the occupier decides, not the injured person.

### 2.10 First aid — chemical splash

**To the eye:**

1. Go to the eyewash immediately. Do not finish the garment first.
2. Hold the eyelids open and flush with gentle running water for at least
   **20 minutes**, longer for alkali. Roll the eye to flush under the lids.
3. Remove contact lenses after the first minute of flushing if they have not
   already washed out.
4. Seek medical attention for every chemical eye splash without exception. Take
   the Safety Data Sheet with you.

**To the skin:**

1. Flush with running water for at least 15 minutes.
2. Remove contaminated clothing while flushing.
3. Do not attempt to neutralise an acid with an alkali or an alkali with an acid
   on skin. The heat of reaction makes the injury worse.
4. Seek medical attention for any blistering, any burn to the face, or any
   contact with rust remover or with oxygen bleach at working strength.

**Swallowed:**

1. Do not induce vomiting.
2. Rinse the mouth with water. Give water to drink if the person is conscious.
3. Ring the Poisons Information Centre on **13 11 26** and follow their
   direction. Have the Safety Data Sheet in your hand when you ring.

**Inhaled:**

1. Move the person to fresh air away from the board.
2. If breathing is difficult, ring 000.
3. Do not send the person back to the board for the rest of the shift.

An eyewash station and a first aid kit must be within 10 seconds' unobstructed
travel of the SB-40. The eyewash must be flushed weekly and the flush recorded.

## 3. Services required

### 3.1 Steam

All three machines take saturated steam from the plant boiler through a common
DN32 header, dropped to each machine through an isolation valve, a strainer and
a separator. Each machine has its own trap set discharging to the common DN25
condensate return.

Steam must be dry saturated. Wet steam will spot a garment at the SA-90 and will
spit water out of the SB-40 gun onto the exact area being spotted. If the steam
is wet, the cause is upstream: a flooded main, a failed separator, a boiler
carrying over, or a trap that has failed closed.

**NOTE.** Steam supply pressure at the header must not fall below 400 kPa while
two machines are calling for steam at once. Below 400 kPa the SA-90 will not
produce a full steam phase and will eventually log SA-01.

### 3.2 Condensate

Each machine has a float-thermostatic trap sized for its own load. The trap set
comprises isolation valve, strainer, trap, sight check and non-return valve into
the common return. The return line falls at not less than 1:100 back to the feed
tank.

Do not manifold two traps into one branch without a non-return valve on each.
The SB-40 trap is much smaller than the other two and will be back-pressured by
the SA-90 if this is done, which puts water in the gun.

### 3.3 Compressed air

Compressed air is taken from the plant receiver through a DN15 branch to a
filter-regulator-lubricator set at each machine. The SB-40 air gun branch must
be taken from the **filtered, unlubricated** leg. Oil carried through to the air
gun will be blown into the garment.

Air must be dry to a pressure dew point of at least 3 °C at line pressure. Water
in the SB-40 air gun will make a ring on every garment it touches.

### 3.4 Vacuum

Vacuum at the SB-40 is generated by the machine's own integral side-channel
blower. No external vacuum service is required. The blower exhausts through the
lint screen and the filter element into the machine base and then to the local
exhaust duct.

### 3.5 Electrical

Each machine has its own isolator within 2 m and in line of sight. The SA-90 and
FF-300 are three-phase; the SB-40 is single-phase and must be RCD-protected as
required by 2.7.

**TECHNICIAN.** Phase rotation matters for the SA-90 fan and the FF-300 blower.
Reversed rotation gives reduced airflow, poor finishing, and eventually a heater
over-temperature trip. Check rotation at every reconnection.

## 5. Diamant SA-90 steam-air finisher

### 5.1 Description

The Diamant SA-90 is a single-station enclosed cabinet finisher. A garment is
hung on a powered hanger inside a steel cabinet, tensioned from the hem, and
subjected in sequence to a saturated steam phase, a hot air phase and a cool air
phase. The purpose of the machine is to relax the fibre, remove creases set by
handling and by the dry cleaning machine, restore the garment's shape, and dry
and set it in that shape before it is unloaded.

The cabinet is a welded steel enclosure with a stainless steel liner, insulated
on all four walls and the roof with 25 mm mineral wool. The front is a
counterweighted door with a laminated glass panel. The floor of the cabinet is a
sloped stainless tray draining to the condensate tundish through a 40 mm trap.

The steam manifold sits behind a perforated diffuser plate on the rear wall.
Twelve nozzles distribute steam across the full working height. A separator and
a strainer upstream of the solenoid keep condensate out of the manifold.

The hot air battery sits above the cabinet roof. The 0.75 kW centrifugal fan
draws room air through a washable inlet filter, over the 9.8 kW heater bank, and
delivers it down the rear plenum and out through the same diffuser plate. The
two heater stages are switched independently by the controller so that the
machine can hold low air temperatures for delicate work without cycling the full
bank.

The controller is a 24-programme unit with a four-line display and a keypad.
Programmes 1 to 12 are factory-set and can be edited but not deleted;
programmes 13 to 24 are free.

### 5.2 The tensioning system

The hanger is fixed. Tension is applied downwards from a hem clamp assembly that
runs on a linear rail at the bottom of the cabinet. A geared 24 V DC motor drives
the clamp down against a load cell until the programmed tension force is
reached, then holds it under closed-loop control for the whole cycle.

Tension is programmed as a percentage of full scale. The relationship is linear:

| Setting | Force |
|---|---|
| 0% | 120 N |
| 25% | 190 N |
| 50% | 260 N |
| 55% | 274 N |
| 75% | 330 N |
| 100% | 400 N |

Tension does what a presser's hands do on a form: it takes out the horizontal
wrinkling that steam alone will not remove, and it holds the length of the
garment while the fibre is plastic. Too little tension and the garment comes out
with a soft, unfinished body. Too much and you will stretch the hem, mark the
fabric at the clamp, and on knitwear you will change the garment's size.

**GARMENT RISK.** Do not use tension above 40% on knitwear, above 35% on silk,
or above 30% on any garment with elastane in the ground weave. These fabrics do
not recover.

The clamp pads are silicone-faced, part TV-SA-4210. Replace them when the
silicone glazes; a glazed pad slips and then grips suddenly, which is how hems
get marked.

### 5.3 Shoulder and sleeve expanders

The **shoulder expanders** are two pivoting plates carried on the hanger yoke.
They travel outward from the centre by 0 to 60 mm per side, driven by a small
pneumatic cylinder through a scissor linkage. Their job is to fill the shoulder
of a jacket or coat so that the steam does not set a hollow at the shoulder
point. Set the travel so that the shoulder seam of the garment sits flat and the
sleeve head is round; do not set it so far that the shoulder seam is pulled
outboard of the garment's own shoulder line.

The **sleeve expanders** are two inflatable bladders that hang inside the sleeves
from the yoke. They are inflated from the compressed air supply through an
independent regulator, 0.5 to 2.5 bar. They fill the sleeve so that the sleeve is
finished round rather than pressed flat with a crease down each side.

Sleeve expander bladders are part TV-SA-1120 and are supplied as a pair. They
are a consumable. Expect 18 to 30 months in a plant running 1,000 garments a
week. A bladder that will not hold pressure will show as a slow air leak and, in
time, as fault SA-03 during the inflation step.

**NOTE.** Sleeve expanders are not used on shirts, on knitwear or on any
sleeveless garment. Set P8 to 0 for those programmes so that the bladders are
not cycled unnecessarily.

### 5.4 The finishing cycle

The SA-90 cycle has five phases and always runs in this order:

1. **Load and tension.** The operator hangs the garment, closes the door and
   presses start. The hem clamp descends and takes up to the programmed force.
   The shoulder and sleeve expanders deploy. Typical duration 6 to 10 s.
2. **Steam.** The steam solenoid opens for the programmed steam time. Saturated
   steam at 550 kPa enters through the diffuser. The fan runs at 30% during this
   phase to distribute steam without cooling it. The purpose is to raise the
   fibre above its glass transition temperature and let internal stresses relax.
3. **Soak.** The steam solenoid closes and the garment is held, still tensioned,
   with no airflow. This lets the moisture equalise through the thickness of the
   cloth instead of sitting on the face. Typical 3 to 6 s. Wool and heavy coating
   need more soak; thin synthetics need almost none.
4. **Hot air.** The heater bank energises and the fan goes to the programmed
   speed. Heated air at the programmed temperature dries the garment while it is
   still under tension. This is the phase that sets the shape.
5. **Cool.** The heaters de-energise and the fan continues at full speed with
   ambient air. The garment must come down through its glass transition
   temperature while still tensioned, or the shape set in phase 4 will relax as
   soon as the tension is released. Skipping the cool phase is the single most
   common cause of a garment that looks finished at the machine and looks
   unfinished on the rail 20 minutes later.

The clamp releases and the expanders retract at the end of the cool phase. The
door may then be opened.

### 5.7 Loading technique

1. Check the garment against the spotter's ticket. A garment that has come back
   from the SB-40 with an unresolved stain must not be finished until the
   decision at 7.9 has been made and recorded; heat will set the stain.
2. Empty every pocket. A pen left in a breast pocket will burst.
3. Do up the top button or the top hook only. Buttoning a jacket all the way
   will set a drag line from the button stand.
4. Hang the garment square on the hanger. The centre back seam must sit on the
   centre of the yoke. A garment hung 20 mm off centre will finish with one
   shoulder higher than the other and no amount of steam will fix it afterwards.
5. Dress the collar down flat and turn the lapels to their natural roll line.
6. Feed the sleeve expanders into the sleeves before you close the door. Do not
   force a bladder into a narrow sleeve; deflate it, feed it, then inflate.
7. Bring the hem clamp up by hand to the hem, centre it, and let the machine take
   up the tension. Do not clamp a lining separately from the shell.
8. Close the door fully. The interlock must click.
9. Select the programme and press start. Watch the first 5 s. If the tension
   overshoots or the garment shifts, stop and reload.
10. Unload as soon as the cycle finishes. A garment left standing in a warm
    cabinet picks up moisture from the tray as the cabinet cools.

### 5.8 Over-finishing — what it looks like

Over-finishing is too much steam, too much heat, too much time, or too much
tension. It shows as:

- **Shine or glazing** on the face of the cloth, especially at the shoulder,
  elbow, seat and any point where a seam allowance lies underneath. On dark
  worsted this is visible from three metres and it is usually permanent.
- **Seam impressions** — the outline of the seam allowance or the pocket bag
  printed through onto the face.
- **Flattened nap** on velvet, moleskin, corduroy or brushed wool.
- **Stretched hems and cuffs**, and on knitwear a garment that is measurably
  longer than it was.
- **Yellowing** on white silk and on wool, from excess heat.
- **Puckered seams** where the shell and the interlining have shrunk at
  different rates.
- **A garment that is damp in the lining and dry on the face**, because the air
  time was increased to force a result that the steam time caused.

The correction is nearly always to reduce P1 and P3 together, not one of them.

### 5.9 Under-finishing — what it looks like

- **Residual body wrinkling** through the back and the sleeve, particularly the
  horizontal wrinkling from the cleaning machine drum.
- **A collapsed shoulder line** or a sleeve head that is not round.
- **Creases that come back** within 20 minutes on the rail. This is a cool-phase
  failure, not a steam failure. Increase P5 before you touch anything else.
- **Damp garments**, which then mark on the conveyor and go out smelling stale.
- **A soft, limp body** on a tailored garment, meaning tension was too low.

If a garment comes out under-finished, do not simply run it again on the same
programme. Two identical inadequate cycles put twice the moisture into the
garment for the same result. Change the programme.

## 6. Princess FF-300 form finisher

### 6.1 Description

The Princess FF-300 is a form finisher, commonly called a Suzy. The garment is
dressed onto a fabric-covered form which is then inflated with heated air from
inside. Where the SA-90 blows air past a garment hanging in a cabinet, the
FF-300 blows air through the garment from the inside out, so the garment is
finished against its own tension rather than against a press.

The machine consists of a steel pedestal, a 1.5 kW centrifugal blower, a 6.0 kW
two-stage heater battery, a steam ring at the base of the form, an inflatable
form on an adjustable shoulder yoke, two sleeve bladders, a collar clamp, and a
pair of hem clamps on a vertically adjustable rail. The control pedestal carries
the display, keypad, foot pedal and the front emergency stop.

Air path: the blower draws room air through a washable filter, passes it over
the heater battery, and delivers it into the base of the form. The form fabric
is permeable; air passes through the form, through the garment, and out. Steam
is injected into the same plenum during the pre-steam phase so that the garment
is steamed from the inside before the hot air starts.

### 6.2 The form

The form is a shaped, air-permeable fabric bag on a wire and plate frame. The
standard cover is TV-FF-1000; a jacket-profile cover with a firmer shoulder and
a longer body is TV-FF-1001. The cover is a consumable and is the single part
that most affects finish quality.

Form inflation pressure is programmed in kilopascals, 0.8 to 4.5 kPa, with a
relief valve set at 5.0 kPa. Inflation is what holds the garment out. Too little
and the garment is finished slack, with wrinkling left in the body. Too much and
the garment is stretched — on a shirt the buttons gape and the button stand
distorts; on a jacket the front edges bow outwards and will not sit closed
afterwards.

Cover condition matters. A cover that has hardened, glazed or gone stiff at the
shoulder will no longer pass air evenly and will print its own seams onto the
inside of a light garment. Replace the cover on condition (see 8.7), typically
every 9 to 15 months at 1,000 garments a week.

### 6.3 Clamping and tensioning

- **Hem clamps.** Two spring-loaded clamps on a vertical rail, adjustable from
  620 mm to 840 mm below the shoulder plate. They pull the garment down against
  the inflation. Programmed as a percentage, F7; 100% is approximately 90 N per
  clamp.
- **Collar clamp.** A pivoting bar that closes over the collar band and holds the
  neck down onto the shoulder yoke. Used on shirts and on anything with a stand
  collar. Not used on a jacket with a rolled collar.
- **Shoulder plates.** Adjustable 380 mm to 520 mm across. Set to the garment's
  own shoulder width, measured seam to seam. This is the most important single
  setting on the machine.
- **Sleeve bladders.** Inflated separately, 0.5 to 3.0 kPa, part TV-FF-1010.

### 6.4 Jacket versus shirt on the form

They are not the same job and the settings are not close to each other.

A **shirt** is a single layer of woven cotton or blend with no interlining except
in the collar and cuffs. It wants high airflow, moderate inflation, high
temperature and a short cycle. The collar clamp is on. The hem clamps are set
firm so the shirt tail is pulled taut. The finish you are after is flat and dry;
a shirt off the form still needs its collar, cuffs and front placket done on the
shirt unit or a press.

A **jacket** is a built garment: shell, interlining, canvas or fusible, and a
lining that is cut to a different length from the shell on purpose. It wants
moderate airflow, low inflation, lower temperature and a longer cycle with a
proper cool phase. The collar clamp is off. The hem clamps are set light, because
a jacket hem pulled hard will drag the lining down and break the lining's ease
pleat. The finish you are after is round and soft, with the roll line of the
lapel preserved.

If you run a jacket on a shirt programme you will get a flat, over-inflated
jacket with bowed fronts and flattened lapels. If you run a shirt on a jacket
programme you will get a damp shirt.

### 6.7 Avoiding seam ripple and shoulder distortion

**Seam ripple** is a wave along a seam, most often the side seam, the armhole or
the shoulder seam of a shirt. It is caused by the two sides of the seam being
held at different tensions while the fibre is plastic. On the FF-300 the causes,
in the order you should check them, are:

1. **Inflation too high for the garment.** The seam thread does not stretch as
   much as the cloth. Reduce F1 by 0.4 kPa and run again.
2. **Hem clamps not level.** If one clamp is set 15 mm lower than the other, one
   side seam carries the load. Level the clamps.
3. **Garment dressed off-centre**, so one shoulder is over the plate edge.
4. **Shoulder plates set wider than the garment**, stretching the shoulder seam
   across the plate ends.
5. **Cool time too short.** The seam relaxes unevenly once released.
6. **A worn form cover** with a hardened patch that does not pass air.

**Shoulder distortion** is a shoulder line that comes out square when it should
be sloped, or a divot at the shoulder point. Causes:

- shoulder plates too wide — the most common cause by a long way. Measure the
  garment shoulder seam to seam and set F10 to that figure, not to the last
  garment's figure;
- shoulder plates too narrow, giving a collapsed, rounded shoulder;
- collar clamp engaged on a garment with a rolled collar, which pulls the neck
  down and lifts the shoulder points;
- inflation too high with a light garment, which balloons the shoulder.

**NOTE.** If a garment comes off with both seam ripple and a distorted shoulder,
the cause is almost always the shoulder plate width. Fix that first and re-run.

### 6.8 Garments with shoulder pads

Shoulder pads are foam or wadding sewn or fused into the shoulder. They compress,
they take up moisture, and they do not give it back at the same rate as the
shell.

- Use programme 09. Reduce inflation (F1 = 1.5 kPa) so the pad is not pushed
  out of its position while wet.
- Increase cool time to 28 s. A pad that is released warm and damp will hold
  whatever shape it was in.
- Feel the pad through the shell at unload. If it is warm and damp, the cycle
  was too short; add 10 s to F5 rather than raising the temperature.
- **GARMENT RISK.** Foam pads in garments more than about fifteen years old
  become brittle and can crumble under steam. If a pad feels crunchy through the
  shell, do not run the garment on the form. Finish it by hand and note it on
  the ticket.
- A pad that has shifted inside its pocket will not be corrected by the form.
  It must be re-seated by the alterations hand before finishing.

### 6.9 Lined jackets

A lining is cut with ease — deliberate slack — at the back and at the hem, and
it is usually a different fibre from the shell. Acetate and viscose linings
behave differently from a wool shell under steam.

- Dress the jacket so the lining hangs freely inside. Do not pull the lining
  down with the shell into the hem clamps. Clamp the shell only.
- Keep F7 at or below 40%. A hard hem pull is what breaks the lining's ease
  pleat at the centre back, and once broken it will show as a drag line for the
  life of the garment.
- Keep F4 at or below 80 °C. Acetate linings glaze above about 90 °C at the
  fabric face and the glaze is permanent.
- Check the lining at unload by opening the front. A lining that is still damp
  when the shell is dry means the airflow is being blocked — check the form
  cover and the inlet filter.
- If a lining bubbles or ripples where it is fused to a fusible interlining, the
  bond has failed. Stop. Steam will make it worse. Refer the garment to
  alterations.

## 7. Spotmaster SB-40 spotting board

### 7.1 Description

The Spotmaster SB-40 is a vacuum spotting board. It is the position at which a
stain is identified, classified and removed before the garment goes to cleaning
or to finishing. Everything in this section assumes the spotter has already
identified the fibre and checked for colour-fastness (see 7.3 and 7.7).

The board comprises:

- a **main board**, 1,400 × 800 mm, foam-padded and covered with a replaceable
  nylon cover (TV-SB-1200 cover, TV-SB-1210 padding);
- a **vacuum table**, 620 × 420 mm of perforated stainless steel let into the
  board, connected to the integral 1.1 kW side-channel blower through a lint
  screen and a filter element;
- a removable **arm board**, 900 × 380 mm, for sleeves and trouser legs;
- a **steam gun**, regulated to 350 kPa, 1.6 mm nozzle;
- an **air gun**, 620 kPa, 2.0 mm nozzle;
- **two chemical guns**, each drawing from a 1.0 L bottle and delivering a fine
  spray at 200 kPa;
- a **rinse pot** and cold water tap;
- a **task lamp**, 18 W LED at 5,000 K giving 1,200 lux at board centre;
- a **local exhaust hood** at the rear, 850 m³/h.

**The vacuum table** is the working part of the board. Vacuum pulls the loosened
stain and the flushing medium down through the fabric and out of the garment
rather than letting it spread sideways. Spotting without vacuum drives the stain
into the fabric and produces a ring. The vacuum is operated by a foot pedal and
must be running during every flush, every steam application and every dry.

**The steam gun** flushes with saturated steam. Steam does three things: it
carries chemistry into the fabric, it flushes the loosened stain out, and it
adds heat which speeds most reactions. Hold the gun 100 to 150 mm from the
fabric, at a shallow angle, and move it. A gun held still and close will wet the
fabric through and will scald the operator's hand.

**The air gun** dries the treated area and feathers the edge of a wet patch so
that no ring is left. Use it with the vacuum on, moving outward from the centre.

**The two chemical guns** are normally charged as gun 1 = wet side, gun 2 = dry
side. Each gun bottle must be labelled (see 7.10). Never swap the contents of a
gun without flushing it (see 8.8).

### 7.2 Wet side and dry side

Spotting chemistry divides into two families.

**Wet side** agents are water-based. They remove stains that are water-soluble
or that respond to water-borne acids, alkalis, enzymes or oxidisers: sugar,
protein, tannin, dye, salt, most food. Wet-side work must be finished with a
neutral rinse and a full dry, or the residual chemistry will keep working in the
garment.

**Dry side** agents are solvent-based. They remove stains that are oil-soluble:
grease, oil, wax, tar, lipstick, some inks, some paints. Dry-side work is done
before the garment goes into the dry cleaning machine, not after.

The rule is: **work the dry side first, then the wet side.** If a stain has both
an oily and a water-soluble component — lipstick, foundation, chocolate, gravy —
remove the oil first with a dry-side agent, then take the residual colour with
wet-side chemistry. Doing it in the other order sets the oil.

### 7.3 Test before you treat

Every agent is tested on a hidden part of the garment before it goes on the
stain. The hidden part is an inside seam allowance, the inside of a hem, a
pocket bag, or the underside of a collar.

1. Apply one drop of the agent to the hidden area.
2. Blot with a white cloth. If colour transfers to the white cloth, the garment
   is not fast to that agent. Stop.
3. Steam-flush the test area and dry it with the air gun. Check for a ring, a
   colour change, a texture change or a loss of finish.
4. If in any doubt, do not proceed. Go to 7.9.

**GARMENT RISK.** A colour-fastness test takes 40 seconds. A dye that has been
stripped off a lapel cannot be put back.

### 7.5 Standard procedure

1. Identify the fibre from the care label. If there is no label, see 7.8 and
   7.9.
2. Identify the stain. If you cannot identify it, treat it as unknown: neutral
   lubricant, dry side first, then wet side, gently.
3. Test the agent (7.3).
4. Lay the stain face down on the vacuum table where possible so the stain is
   pushed out the way it came in.
5. Start the vacuum.
6. Apply the agent sparingly. More agent is not faster.
7. Apply the permitted mechanical action (7.6) for the fibre, not for the stain.
8. Steam-flush from the outside of the stain inward.
9. Repeat from step 6 up to three times. If three applications of the correct
   agent have not moved the stain, change the approach or stop.
10. Neutralise: acid work is followed by a dilute alkaline rinse, alkaline work
    by a dilute acid rinse (TVC-11), then a clear water flush.
11. Dry with the air gun, feathering outward, vacuum running.
12. Record the treatment if the garment is at risk (7.9, Appendix D).

**Mechanical action** on this board means: the bone or spatula (light scraping
and tamping), the brush (tamping only, never scrubbing), and the fingers. There
is no fourth option. Rubbing a stain between the fingers is not a technique; it
is how chafing happens.

### 7.7 Colour-fastness and pre-checks

Before any treatment: check the care label; check for a fugitive dye by the drop
test at 7.3; check for existing damage, thin patches and open seams; check
trims, buttons and beads for solvent sensitivity; check for a previous
unsuccessful home treatment, which is the single most common reason a stain will
not respond.

Record anything found on the ticket before you start. A garment that arrives
with a hole does not become the plant's hole because nobody wrote it down.

### 7.8 Fibre-by-fibre cautions

**Wool.** Alkali-sensitive. Protein formula and ammonia will damage the fibre if
left in contact. Neutralise every alkaline treatment. Never use hot water and
mechanical action together — that is felting, and it is irreversible. Maximum
tamping: light. Chlorine bleach must never be used on wool in any circumstances.

**Silk.** The most fragile common fibre on the board. Alkali-sensitive like
wool, and additionally prone to water marking, dye bleeding and loss of lustre.
Use minimum moisture, minimum agent, minimum mechanical action, and feather
every edge. Steam at a distance. Never use oxygen bleach on coloured silk.

**Acetate.** Dissolved by acetone and by amyl acetate. TVC-05 will make a hole
in an acetate lining in seconds, not minutes. Heat-sensitive: glazes above about
90 °C. Water-marks readily. Test everything.

**Triacetate.** More heat- and solvent-resistant than acetate but still
dissolved by amyl acetate. Treat as acetate for chemical purposes.

**Rayon / viscose.** Loses about half its strength when wet. Do not tamp wet
viscose. Prone to water marking, to shrinkage, and to permanent distortion if
tensioned wet. Use minimum moisture and dry it flat and quickly.

**Cotton.** Tolerant of most chemistry and of mechanical action. Weakened by
acids: rust remover left in cotton will produce a hole at the next wash.
Neutralise after every acid treatment. Prone to ring formation, so feather
carefully.

**Linen.** As cotton chemically, but the fibre is stiff and brittle when dry.
Over-tamping breaks the fibre and produces a permanent light patch that looks
like abrasion because it is abrasion.

**Polyester.** Chemically the most tolerant fibre. Its problem is oil: polyester
is oleophilic and holds grease stains hard. Expect several dry-side passes.
Heat-set creases in polyester will not steam out.

**Nylon.** Attacked by strong acids, so rust remover is a real risk. Scavenges
loose dye out of solution readily, which is why a nylon garment picks up dye
transfer in a mixed load. Yellows with heat and with age.

**Elastane / spandex.** Present in small percentages in a great many modern
garments. Destroyed by chlorine bleach. Degraded by heat above about 110 °C and
by repeated high-temperature finishing. If a garment has lost its stretch, the
elastane has already gone and no treatment will restore it.

**Leather and suede trim.** Do not spot leather trim on this board. Water marks
it, solvent strips its finish, and steam shrinks and hardens it. Mask the trim,
work around it, or send the garment to a leather specialist.

**Bonded and laminated fabrics.** Two layers joined by adhesive. Steam, solvent
and heat all attack the adhesive. Bubbling or lifting means the bond has failed
and it cannot be repaired. Test at a hidden edge. If the fabric shows any sign
of separation, stop.

**Beaded, sequinned and printed garments.** Many sequins and most heat-transfer
prints are dissolved or lifted by dry-side solvent and by amyl acetate. Test on
a single hidden sequin. If it clouds, softens or loses colour, stop.

### 7.9 When not to spot

Some garments must go back to the customer untouched. The decision to refuse
treatment is a professional judgement and it protects both the garment and the
business. Refuse treatment where:

- **there is no care label and the fibre cannot be identified with confidence.**
  Without a fibre, no agent can be chosen safely;
- **the dye is not fast** to water, to the required agent, or to solvent, on the
  drop test at 7.3;
- **the fabric is bonded or laminated** and shows any sign of separation;
- **the garment is already damaged** — thin, split, brittle, sun-degraded, or
  showing broken fibres at the stain itself. Chemistry will finish what the
  damage started;
- **the stain has been treated at home** with an unknown product, particularly a
  chlorine bleach or a proprietary stain stick. The residue may react with the
  board chemistry;
- **the stain is dye damage, not a stain** — a colour change with no deposit on
  the surface. There is nothing to remove;
- **the garment is a leather, suede, fur or fur-trimmed article**;
- **the garment carries an owner's instruction not to treat it**;
- **the value or sentiment attached to the garment is out of proportion to the
  chance of success** — a wedding dress, a christening gown, an heirloom, a
  costume. These go to a specialist or go back untreated.

**How the decision is recorded.** Where treatment is refused or limited, the
spotter completes the Spotting Record at Appendix D before the garment leaves
the board. The record captures the ticket number, the garment, the stain, the
fibre (or "unidentified"), the reason for refusal by reference to the list
above, the tests performed, the spotter's name and the date. A copy of the
relevant lines is written onto the customer's docket, and the counter staff are
told so that the customer is advised at collection and not after it.

Where treatment proceeds on an at-risk garment, the same record is completed
with the customer's acknowledgement noted, and the record is retained for not
less than 12 months.

### 7.10 Chemical safety, storage, decanting and labelling

**Storage.** Keep the chemistry set in a dedicated, ventilated, lockable cabinet
in the finishing room, not under the board and not on the board. Acids and
alkalis on separate shelves, oxidiser (TVC-06) on its own shelf, flammables
(TVC-05, TVC-09) in a flammable liquids cabinet. Maximum quantity on the board
at any time: one 1.0 L gun bottle per gun plus the working bottles in use.

**Decanting.** Decant only into a labelled container of the same material as the
original. Decant under the exhaust hood with the hood running, with goggles and
gloves on, and over the drip tray. Never decant into a drink bottle, a food
container or an unlabelled bottle. Never top up a part-used bottle from a
different batch without confirming it is the same product.

**Labelling.** Every container on the board, including the two gun bottles,
carries a label showing the product name, the TVC part number, the hazard
pictograms, the signal word, and the date it was filled. A container without a
label is emptied to waste, not used. Do not rely on colour coding alone.

**Safety Data Sheets.** The occupier must hold a current SDS for every product
on the board. An SDS more than five years old must be replaced by the supplier's
current version. The SDS register at Appendix C lists each product, its supplier,
the SDS issue date and its review date. The register is checked quarterly. SDSs
must be readily accessible to any worker at the board — a folder within reach,
not a file on a computer that only the office can open.

**Spills.** Small spills on the board: flush with water and vacuum through.
Spills on the floor: absorb with the spill kit, bag the absorbent, and dispose
of it as prescribed industrial waste through the waste contractor. Do not wash
solvent or concentrated chemistry to the trade waste drain.

**Waste.** Board waste water goes to trade waste under the site's trade waste
agreement. Concentrated residues, empty solvent containers and contaminated
absorbent go out as prescribed industrial waste. Rags used with dry-side agents
are stored in a lidded metal bin, not in an open bin and not in a plastic bag.

### 8.6 Steam trap testing

Test each trap monthly with a contact thermometer and by ear.

- **Working correctly:** intermittent discharge, inlet temperature at saturation
  for the line pressure (about 160 °C at 550 kPa), outlet noticeably cooler.
- **Failed open (blowing through):** continuous flow, inlet and outlet at the
  same temperature, an audible continuous hiss. Wastes steam and pressurises the
  return. Replace.
- **Failed closed (blocked):** trap and inlet cold or barely warm, machine
  performance falls off, water carries over into the cabinet or the gun.
  Replace.

Record the result against the trap identifier in the weekly log. A trap that has
failed twice in twelve months indicates a dirt or waterhammer problem upstream;
fit or clean the strainer before fitting another trap.

### 8.7 Form cover replacement, FF-300

Two people. Isolate as 8.2 and allow the machine to cool. Release the hem clamp
rail and lower it fully. Release the shoulder plate clamps and lift the yoke
clear. Unhook the cover's draw cord at the base, roll the old cover up from the
hem and off over the shoulder frame. Inspect the frame for broken wires and
sharp edges; dress any burrs. Fit the new cover from the shoulder down, seat it
squarely on the shoulder plates, draw and tie the base cord, and check that no
fold is trapped under a plate. Re-assemble, restore services and run three
cycles on programme 06 with no garment to bed the cover in. Record the date and
the part number.

### 8.8 Chemical gun flushing

Weekly, and always before a gun's contents are changed.

1. Hood running, goggles and gloves on, waste pot on the board.
2. Remove the bottle. Cap it and set it aside.
3. Fit a bottle of clean water. Discharge the gun into the waste pot until the
   spray runs clear, then for a further 10 s.
4. Remove the pickup tube and check its filter, TV-SB-3120. Rinse or replace.
5. Discharge air only through the gun for 5 s to clear the passage.
6. Refit the correct bottle, check the label, and test the spray pattern on a
   waste cloth. The pattern must be an even cone; a split or one-sided pattern
   means the nozzle is partly blocked.
7. Dispose of the waste pot contents as prescribed industrial waste.

**CAUTION.** A gun that has held an acid formula must be flushed with water and
then with dilute alkaline rinse before it is charged with an alkaline formula,
and vice versa. Residue in the gun body is enough to gas off in the bottle.

### 9.2 Troubleshooting the eight most common faults

**SA-01 / FF-01 steam pressure low.** Almost always a plant problem, not a
machine problem. Check the boiler gauge first. If the boiler is at pressure and
the machine is not, the fault is between them: a closed or partly closed
isolation valve, a blocked strainer, or a failed-closed trap flooding the drop
leg. If SA-01 and FF-01 appear together whenever both machines cycle at once,
the steam supply is undersized or the header is too small; see the note at 3.7.
A machine that raises SA-01 only on the first cycle of the morning is telling
you the header has not been warmed through; warm the line before the first
garment rather than adjusting P12.

**SA-04 / FF-05 airflow and blower faults.** In nine cases out of ten the inlet
filter is blocked with lint. Wash it, dry it, refit it and restart. If the fault
returns with a clean filter, listen for the fan: if it runs but the machine
still faults, the airflow switch or its sensing tube has failed; if it does not
run, check for a tripped overload. After any electrical work, reversed phase
rotation gives a fan that runs, sounds normal and moves almost no air — this
presents as poor finishing before it presents as a fault code.

**SA-09 / FF-08 over-temperature.** The cut-out has tripped because the heaters
were on without enough air over them. Find out why before resetting. Blocked
filter, failed fan, closed damper, or a welded heater contactor. A contactor
that has welded closed will trip the cut-out again immediately after reset and
must be replaced. Do not defeat or bypass the cut-out under any circumstances.
Two over-temperature trips in a week is a machine that should be taken out of
service until a technician has attended.

**SA-10 / FF-09 cycle timeout.** The controller has not seen a phase complete.
Note which phase the display was showing. Timeout during steam usually means low
steam pressure that has not yet reached the SA-01 threshold. Timeout during hot
air usually means the plenum never reached setpoint, which is a heater or sensor
problem. Timeout during tension or inflation is mechanical: on the SA-90 a
dirty rail or a fouled garment, on the FF-300 a cover that will not hold.

**FF-03 form inflation failure.** Check in this order: the base draw cord is
tied and seated; the cover has no tear, particularly at the shoulder plate
corners where it wears first; the sleeve bladder connections are made; the
blower runs. A cover that has stretched with age will inflate slowly and fail
intermittently — this is the point at which it should be replaced rather than
patched. If the cover is sound and the blower runs, check that the relief valve
has not stuck open, which will vent the plenum and produce the same symptom.

**SB-01 vacuum low.** Clear the lint screen first; it takes 30 seconds and it is
the cause most of the time. Then wash the filter element. A filter element that
has been washed more than about ten times will no longer come back and should be
replaced. If both are clean and depression is still below 11 kPa, look for a
leak: the arm board seal, an open access panel, or a split in the duct to the
blower. A vacuum that is weak only at one end of the table is a partial blockage
under the perforated plate, not a blower fault.

**SB-03 steam gun no flow.** Purge the gun into the waste pot. If it spits water
and then runs, the drop leg was flooded and the trap should be tested. If
nothing comes out at all, the nozzle is blocked with scale; remove and clean it
with the pin, and if scale is recurring, the boiler water treatment is the real
problem. If the gun runs but weakly and the line is hot, the regulator has
failed and is a technician item. Never enlarge a blocked nozzle with a drill;
the orifice size sets the gun's flushing behaviour.

**SB-05 / SB-06 chemical gun blocked.** Usually crystallised product in the
nozzle or the pickup filter, from a gun left charged over a weekend. Flush per
8.8. If the gun blocks again within the week, the bottle contents have been
mixed or contaminated: empty it, flush the gun fully, and refill from a sealed
supply container. Never clear a chemical gun nozzle by blowing back through it
with your mouth or by pressing it against a rag and firing.

## 11. Warranty and service

### 11.1 Terms

Trevil warrants each machine against defect in materials and workmanship for
**24 months on parts and 12 months on labour**, from the date of commissioning
recorded in Appendix A. Warranty is transferable with the machine on written
notice. A warranty claim must quote the machine serial number and the fault code
displayed, and must be supported by the maintenance log for the preceding
12 months.

### 11.2 Exclusions

Warranty does not cover: consumable and wear parts, being form covers, board
covers and padding, all bladders, all filters and lint screens, all nozzles,
seals, gaskets, clamp pads, hoses and lamps; damage caused by steam quality,
including scale, carryover and wet steam; damage caused by air quality,
including water and oil carried through the compressed air supply; corrosion
from spotting chemistry; damage caused by supply voltage outside ±6% of nominal,
by phase loss or by reversed rotation; damage from freezing; garments; and any
consequential loss.

### 11.3 What voids the warranty

- operating a machine with a guard, interlock or safety device removed,
  bypassed or defeated, including setting P14 to 0 on the SA-90;
- resetting an over-temperature cut-out repeatedly without correcting the cause;
- using the SB-40 with chemistry other than that at 7.4, or with any product for
  which no SDS is held;
- work inside a control enclosure by a person who is not a licensed electrician
  or a Trevil-authorised technician;
- modification of the steam, air, drain or exhaust connections away from the
  data at Section 3, including joining the SB-40 hood into a common duct;
- failure to carry out the scheduled maintenance at Section 8, evidenced by the
  absence of the log sheets;
- relocation of a machine without re-commissioning.

### 11.4 Service

Scheduled service is by a Trevil-authorised technician at not more than
12-month intervals, and includes the quarterly and annual tasks at 8.3, 8.4 and
8.5. A service report must be issued and filed with this manual. Faults marked Y
in the table at 9.1 require an authorised technician; an operator may not clear
them.
