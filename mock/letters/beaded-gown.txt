To Whom It May Concern,

We confirm that the garment described below was professionally cleaned at our premises on 10 June 2026 on behalf of our mutual customer Marguerite Oakley, under our docket B-24118. The garment is a Carla Zampatti navy beaded evening gown, full length with cap sleeves and all-over bugle beading, purchased from your store in October 2023. We have cleaned the same gown twice before, in October 2024 and December 2025, on both occasions without incident.

The care label is intact and legible, sewn into the left side seam. It reads "Dry clean only" and carries a circle containing the letter "P" with a bar beneath it, being a reduced perchloroethylene process. It carries no supplementary warning of any kind relating to the beading. We did not use perchloroethylene. The gown was cleaned in hydrocarbon solvent on our Realstar HM-450, programme 6, which is our beaded and at-risk programme: a four minute bath, no spin above 200 rpm, and drying at 38 degrees C with an extended cool-down. It was bagged in a net with the beading turned inward, run alone rather than with other work, and it was not pressed at any stage. Hydrocarbon on that programme is materially milder than the process the label permits. We selected it because the beads are unlabelled and test as plastic rather than glass, and plastic bugle beads are the component most at risk in any solvent.

On unloading, our plant operator recorded that approximately 15 per cent of the bugle beads across the bodice had lost surface clarity and gone matt. No beads were lost. None of the securing stitching has failed. The polyester ground fabric is entirely unaffected: there is no shrinkage, no distortion, no colour change, no solvent ring and no loss of hand. The change is confined to the surface of the beads themselves, and it is denser on the bodice, where the beading is dense, than on the skirt, where it thins out.

That distribution is the diagnosis. A cleaning fault acts on a garment as a whole and shows first in the ground fabric, the seams and the interlinings. Here the ground is perfect and only one component has altered. The bugle beads carry an applied surface coating, which is what gives a plastic bead its clarity and depth of colour. That coating is bonded to the bead by the bead manufacturer and is not a fabric finish we can test for or condition. Where the bond is weak or the coating has been thinly applied, solvent immersion will craze and lift it, and the bead goes matt while everything around it stays sound. That is a component failure, not a process failure.

DLI TABS — Prints, Embellishments, Trims & Notions addresses this failure mode directly. It records that coated and surface-finished beads, sequins and applied trims may dull, craze, delaminate or dissolve in any professional solvent, that the behaviour depends on the coating chemistry chosen by the trim supplier, and that it cannot be predicted or prevented by a cleaner who has followed the care instructions provided. There is no test available at the counter that distinguishes a sound bead coating from an unsound one. The International Fair Claims Guide for Consumer Textile Products places responsibility for a component that fails in a process the label permits with the party that selected and specified that component, which is the manufacturer, not the cleaner.

We would also draw your attention to the care labelling. AS/NZS 1957 Textiles — Care Labelling requires that care instructions account for the whole garment, including trim and applied decoration, and that any component requiring different or restricted treatment be identified. This label does not mention the beading at all. Had it carried a warning that the trim was not solvent-stable, or specified a process on account of the beads, the garment would have been declined or handled differently. In our view the inadequacy of the instructions has contributed materially to the outcome.

In summary: the gown was cleaned strictly in accordance with its care label, by a process gentler than that label permits, with every professional precaution available to us, by a plant that has cleaned this garment successfully twice before. It is our professional opinion that the change to the beads results from the failure of the coating applied to the beads at manufacture, and not from the cleaning process.

We have advised Mrs Oakley to return the gown to the original point of purchase for assessment, and we have released it to her for that purpose. Our intake photographs, our post-cycle photographs and the machine record for the cycle are held on file.

If it would assist your assessment or that of the manufacturer, we are happy to provide those records, the full cycle parameters, or a further technical explanation, and you are welcome to contact us directly.
