Dear Alana,

The discolouration you found when you opened the box on 8 August was caused by the way we packed your gown, and we accept responsibility for it. You paid us for a clean and preserve service and the preserving half of it was not done properly.

The clean itself was sound. The gown was cleaned on 11 February in hydrocarbon on our bridal programme, alone, with the hem mark reduced by hand on the spotting board, and the residual hem mark was recorded on the docket and shown to you when you collected. It went into the box in good order. What went wrong was the packing on 13 February. Our preserve service is sold on acid-free, unbuffered tissue and an archival box, and this gown was interleaved with ordinary white tissue from older stock. Ordinary tissue is made with residual acidity and it releases acidic gases as it ages in a closed box. Over six months against silk, that produces exactly what you have — a broad yellow-brown discolouration following the fold lines, worst on the bodice and down the right skirt panel where the fabric was folded hardest. We have kept the tissue and photographed it with the box and the gown together. DLI TABS — Colour Fading & Discoloration describes this as acid migration from non-archival packaging, and it is a storage fault, not a cleaning fault. It is ours either way.

On the money. Textile claims are settled on the International Fair Claims Guide for Consumer Textile Products, which is the standard the trade, the insurers and Consumer Affairs all work from, and it settles on depreciated value rather than the price of a new one. The Guide gives a wedding gown after the wedding a normal life expectancy of three years. Yours is 10 months old, which puts it in the 10 to 19 month band, and its condition is excellent. That band at excellent condition adjusts to 70 per cent, which on the $3,200 invoiced by Rosalind Bridal would be $2,240.

There is a second step that applies to wedding gowns specifically, and I want to set it out rather than have the number appear from nowhere. The Guide caps a gown after the wedding at 50 per cent of its replacement cost, on the basis that a wedding gown has already served the purpose it was bought for and is not a garment in ongoing use. Half of $3,200 is $1,600, so the 70 per cent adjustment is reduced to the cap. Our offer is $1,600.00. The clean and preserve charge of $295.00 is waived and will be refunded to you in full.

There is something else worth considering before you decide. You may want the gown more than the money. We are willing to attempt a reduction of the discolouration at our own cost — a controlled bleaching and rinse sequence on silk mikado, done in stages with a stop at each one. I will not promise a result. Acid discolouration of this age sometimes comes back a long way and sometimes barely moves, and there is a real risk of losing evenness across the panels. But it costs you nothing to let us try, and if it works you keep your gown. If you would like us to attempt it, we will do the work first and settle afterwards, so you can see the result before you decide.

On payment of the full adjusted value the gown becomes our property, which is the ordinary position under the Guide. If you would rather keep it, whether or not we attempt the reduction, tell us and it stays yours.
