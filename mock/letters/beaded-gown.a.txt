We defend this one, and we defend it confidently.

Three things decide it. The label permits reduced perc and you used hydrocarbon on programme 6 — a four minute bath, no spin above 200 rpm, 38 degrees with an extended cool-down. That is materially gentler than the process the manufacturer authorised, and a cleaner who goes gentler than the label cannot be said to have exceeded it. Second, the damage is confined to one component. The polyester ground is unmarked, unshrunk and unringed and the stitching is intact. A cleaning fault shows in the ground fabric first, every time. When only the beads have changed, the beads are the problem. Third, you have the signed at-risk authorisation from 10 June naming the beading and the possibility of clouding, plus six intake photographs and four after.

The technical basis is DLI TABS — Prints, Embellishments, Trims & Notions. It records that coated and surface-finished beads can dull, craze or delaminate in any professional solvent depending on the coating chemistry the trim supplier chose, and that this cannot be predicted or prevented by a cleaner following the care label. There is no counter test for a bead coating. The International Fair Claims Guide puts responsibility for a component that fails in a label-permitted process with whoever specified the component — the manufacturer.

Worth adding: the label says nothing about the beading at all. AS/NZS 1957 requires care instructions to account for trim, so the labelling is non-compliant, and that is the manufacturer's problem too.
